Surface-To-Air Missiles Deployed To Protect Trump’s Mar-a-Lago Estate

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

U.S. Army Humvee-based AN/TWQ-1Avenger short-range air defense systems (SHORADS) have been set up near President Donald Trump’s Mar-a-Lago estate in Palm Beach, Florida, as well as at Donald J. Trump International Airport, about three miles to the west. The Avengers were deployed about two weeks ago amid heightened concerns about the president’s safety.

Iran threatened Trump’s life long before he ordered the joint attack with Israel on the Islamic Republic in February. Airstrikes wiped out much of Iran’s senior leadership structure at the onset of Operation Epic Fury. Now the American leader is mulling over plans to renew attacks on Iran as early as next week. In addition, Trump has been the target of assassination attempts and threats not connected to Iran. There may also be elevated security concerns surrounding the midterm elections.

Officials would not provide any specific reasons for the presence of the SAM systems when we asked.

“At the request of the U.S. Secret Service (USSS) and under NORAD [North American Aerospace Defense Command] and USNORTHCOM [U.S. Northern Command] authorities, NORAD and USNORTHCOM recently deployed ground-based air defense [GBAD] assets and Counter-Unmanned Aircraft System capabilities in and around Mar-a-Lago, Florida,” the commands told us in a statement. “These assets add a complementary layer of technology to enhance protection. Due to operational security, we are unable to provide additional details. For further detail, we refer you to the USSS.”

“The U.S. Secret Service is operating in a heightened threat environment, and we continuously adapt our protective operations to incorporate cutting-edge tools, technology, and methods to safeguard our protectees,” the Secret Service told us. “At the request of the U.S. Secret Service, NORAD and U.S. Northern Command are providing Counter-Unmanned Aircraft System capabilities in support of our protective operations.”

NORAD/NORTHCOM declined to say what kinds of ground-based air defense assets are involved or where they are set up. However, Getty Images photographs show two Avenger systems set up on a floating platform in Lake Worth Lagoon on the Mar-a-Lago shoreline and one on the beach.

Avenger air defense systems seen at Mar-A-Lago in Palm Beach, Florida, on October 16, 2026, ahead of President Donald Trump’s first landing at the newly renamed President Donald J. Trump International Airport. (Photo by Christopher Beckett/NurPhoto) Christopher Beckett

There are also at least two Avengers at nearby Trump International Airport.

A military service member stands atop an AN/TWQ-1 Avenger air defense systems on the tarmac near a C-17 Globemaster transport aircraft at President Donald J. Trump International Airport (DJT) in West Palm Beach, Florida, on October 15, 2026. (Photo by Christopher Beckett/NurPhoto) Christopher Beckett

The command did confirm to us that it sent its Counter-Unmanned Aircraft System Fly-Away Kits to support these operations. These are rapidly deployable systems to react to drone threats or ongoing incursions consisting of passive and active sensor systems, such as electro-optical and infrared cameras, Ku-band radar, and electronic support measures, to help spot, classify and track drones. Electronic warfare systems can also be deployed as part of these fly-away kits.

A NORTHCOM/NORAD counter-drone Fly-Away kit. (U.S. Military)

The Avenger is a turreted system with two launchers, each capable of holding four Stinger heat-seeking surface-to-air missiles. The Stinger missiles they fire have been upgraded to deal with drones. Avengers are meant for point defense of forces on-the-go but have also played a major homeland defense role since 9/11. One is even hard-mounted on a rooftop near the White House for air defense purposes.

The deployment of these systems illustrates just how seriously the U.S. Secret Service is taking the threat of drones. Drone technology, including first-person view types and those equipped with fiber optics, is rapidly proliferating around the world and Iran has been both a user and distributor of these weapons. The Islamic Republic has uses long-range kamikaze drones, which could be a particular threat, especially considering Trump’s estate’s close proximity to the sea, which offers a most likely launch option. It’s also worth noting that traditional aircraft are also a threat and the Stingers are capable of dealing with them too.

An Army Avenger air defense system stands ready during a NORAD Temporary Flight Restriction enforcement mission in Bedminster, New Jersey, Aug. 9, 2026. (U.S. Air Force photo by Lt. Col. Andrew Scott) Lt. Col. Andrew Scott

These assets are meant to augment the NORAD-enforced temporary flight restrictions (TFRs) over the Mar-a-Lago airspace. There is a standing TFR that runs through Oct. 20, 2027. Additional ones are created when Trump visits the area.

The Temporary Flight Restriction (TFR) imposed over Mar-a-Lago. (FAA)

“More than 160 planes and drones have breached the airspace over Palm Beach since the restrictions took effect in October 2025, according to an Aug. 11 FAA filing in federal court” that was first reported that month by the Palm Beach Daily News.

The most recent publicly announced incursion took place Oct. 24.

“A NORAD F-16 fighter aircraft intercepted a general aviation aircraft over Palm Beach, Florida, at approximately 12:20 p.m. EST today after the aircraft entered a NORAD-enforced FAA Temporary Flight Restriction (TFR) area over Mar-a-Lago,” the command stated in a media release at the time. “After the fighter jet executed a standard headbutt maneuver to gain the pilot’s attention, the civilian aircraft established communication and safely exited the restricted airspace in full compliance with flight instructions.”

These combat air patrols (CAPs) are standard for presidential visits. Even when Trump is at Mar-a-Lago for days, there are usually CAPs flying overhead, which are extremely resource-intensive, as we explained in a previous story about one that took place during Trump’s first term.

The deployments of Avengers to Florida mark at least the second time since August that these systems have been used to protect Trump. Avengers were seen on Aug. 9, when Trump was attending the final round of the LIV Golf Bedminster tournament at his New Jersey golf course.

In that situation, NORAD told us that when deployed to assist the Secret Service in protecting POTUS, the Avenger systems are operated by the 263rd Army Air and Missile Defense Command. The command today declined to say if that is the case with the current deployments.

On Aug. 1, the White House shared a video of Trump talking with soldiers at Bedminster. In the background was an Avenger.

Trump, as we noted earlier in this story, has already been the subject of several assassination attempts and threats.

The most notable came on July 13, 2024, at a campaign event in Butler, Pennsylvania, when a man fired a shot at Trump, then the Republican presidential nominee. Thomas Matthew Crooks, 20, of Bethel Park, Pennsylvania, was killed, and a bloodied Trump was whisked away.

Prior to those attempts, Iran made a golf-related threat against Trump.

An image highly suggestive of a drone targeting the then-former President Trump on a golf course in a “vengeance” strike in retaliation for the death of Iranian General Qassem Soleimani was posted on Twitter by an account for Iran’s then-Supreme Leader, Ayatollah Ali Khamenei. The U.S. military killed Soleimani in a drone strike in Iraq in January 2020, which had already prompted public threats from Iranian officials, including against Trump specifically.

Twitter suspended the @khamenei_site account around 15 hours after the post when the image first appeared on Jan. 21, 2020.

Khamenei himself was killed Feb. 28 during the opening night of Operation Epic Fury.

An X account tied to Iran’s former supreme leader posted this image of a drone looming over Donald Trump as he played golf. (X)

There has been extreme concern for Trump’s safety following the launch of that operation, with an Iranian revenge hit on the president being more plausible now than ever. This has triggered major changes in safety protocols, some of which have made headline news.

Alarms were raised earlier this month after U.S. intelligence found Iran has “‘substantially increased’ its efforts to target American diplomats and officials in the Middle East and beyond, with lethal plots reaching ‘advanced stages,'” Axios reported, citing an Oct. 23 memo to Secretary of State Marco Rubio the outlet obtained.

The Avengers are the most visible indication of increased efforts to protect Trump amongst threats that are unlikely to recede anytime soon.

‘Open the hives’ — Why France is turning tank gunners into FPV pilots

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

MAILLY-LE-CAMP, France — Corporal Igor started a new job this year, swapping the sights of a 105 mm gun on the French Army’s AMX 10 RC reconnaissance vehicle for first-person view goggles and a handheld controller.

The 21-year-old is now a strike pilot in France’s first dedicated drone-combat squadron, crouching in nettles and tall grass as he guided an FPV drone down a forest path at the Mailly-le-Camp training area east of Paris during a June exercise of the 1st Marine Infantry Regiment, or 1er RIMa.

“We’re switching specialties, we’re keeping up with the times,” said the corporal, who last year still manned the gunner seat in the AMX 10 RC armored car.

Inspired by the war in Ukraine, the 1er RIMa converted one of its three light cavalry squadrons into an experimental all-drone unit, with results so convincing that the Army is rushing more drone formations into service. The aim is for around 15 drone-combat units by summer 2027 and more by the end of next year, Army Chief of Staff Gen. Jacques de Montgros said this month.

France deployed a drone-combat unit to Romania for the first time in recent weeks as part of Mission Aigle, the French-led mission to strengthen NATO’s eastern flank, and will deploy a second unit to Poland for an exercise next month, de Montgros told lawmakers in an Oct. 14 hearing. “So we’re really at a point of scaling up our efforts in these areas.”

Cpl. Igor of the French Army’s 1st Marine Infantry Regiment operates an FPV drone during a training exercise at Mailly-le-Camp on June 11, 2026. (Rudy Ruitenberg/staff)

The 1er RIMa’s experimental squadron has provided a blueprint to create more tactical drone formations, while offering the French Army a lesson in the vulnerability of NATO-style combined-arms tactics to drone threats. During exercises this year, the regiment’s pilots hunted armored vehicles and command posts of the opposing force, disrupting maneuver and force buildup.

Mixing surveillance drones, FPV one-way attack drones and loitering munitions, the 1er Escadron de Drones de Chasse, which roughly translates as 1st Hunter-Drone Squadron, has also been a test bed for the Army, partnering with French firms to work on swarming technology, autonomous terminal guidance and launch canisters.

France’s first drone squadron grew from an idea within the 1er RIMa’s training and operations office in late 2023, to develop a way of fighting with drones that went beyond just adding them to existing units. The concept built on a proposal by Capt. Guillaume, an open-source intelligence specialist who had been studying the war in Ukraine.

When the office tested an experimental drone unit in a tactical exercise, the drones created “a crazy amount of disruption,” said Col. Aymeric Caussin, commander of the 1er RIMa, who headed the office at the time. “So we said, right, let’s go for it.”

Cavalry heritage

Drawing on the regiment’s light cavalry heritage, the squadron was designed as a mobile force with drones as its weapons system, said Lt. Col Remi, who worked to implement the idea during a two-year stint in the 1er RIMa. Light cavalry principles still applied: gather intelligence, then fix, outflank and ultimately destroy the enemy, with a focus on mobility, dispersion and stealth.

“We needed a squadron that evoked the light cavalry: ‘drone’ because that’s its primary weapon system, and ‘hunter’ because that’s what we actually do in the field.”

The regiment tested the concept during Exercise Hedgehog in Estonia in May 2025, sending some 10 drone pilots as part of a French combined-arms tactical group. The U.S. 5th squadron, 7th Cavalry Regiment served as the designated opposing force during the exercise, with dismounted soldiers and M2A4 Bradley Fighting Vehicles.

The Wall Street Journal previously reported how Ukrainian operators taking part in the exercise exposed the vulnerability of NATO troops to drone warfare. The French team equally showed the impact of drones on an opponent unprepared for the threat.

The French pilots, who made up around 5% of the tactical group’s active strength, accounted for 20% of the attrition inflicted on the opposing force of American troops with Bradleys in a single day of combat, said Maj. Sylvain, head of operations at the 1er RIMa, who attended the exercise.

The virtual devastation convinced French Army leaders to scale up the experiment. Within two months, the drone squadron was made official, and in early April 2026 the Army staff declared it “fully operational.”

“It was the proof of concept,” Maj. Sylvain said. “Everything falls into place, everyone is supportive of the project, and the regiment decides to transform the first squadron into a hunter-drone squadron.”

The drone team also brought several startups to Estonia for Hedgehog 25, including Alta Ares and Icarus Swarms, while Naval Group joined at its own expense. The 1er RIMa works with Alta Ares on AI-enabled detection and reconnaissance, Icarus Swarms on autonomous navigation and computer-assisted piloting, and Naval Group on drone-launch canisters.

The drone squadron has continue to rack up the virtual kills since Hedgehog. During France’s large-scale exercise Orion 2026, the 1er RIMa’s drone pilots tallied four regimental command posts, a number of logistics convoys and anti-drone and air-defense units, according to Caussin.

‘Quite a bit of damage’

What made the drone-combat unit stand out was its responsiveness, with FPV drones able to quickly close gaps in the lines and attack the main axis of enemy efforts, and its ability to disrupt the enemy command structures, particularly by directly targeting command posts, Caussin said.

“Through the drone, we’ll seek to disrupt the enemy’s chain of command. We’re influencing decision-making.”

Orion 2026 showed that distance no longer protects. The squadron’s intelligence, surveillance and reconnaissance drones detected tactical command posts several kilometers from the front line, then called in artillery or directed FPV drones to attack, said Staff Sgt. Tristan, who leads a platoon of FPV pilots after about 12 years on the AMX 10 RC.

Troops of the French Army’s 1st Marine Infantry Regiment launch an FPV drone from a canister during an exercise at the Mailly-le-Camp training grounds on June 11, 2026. (Rudy Ruitenberg/staff)

The regiment inflicted “quite a bit of damage” with drones and artillery, “but had it been the other way around, it would have been the same,” Staff Sgt. Tristan said. “If we’d been facing a unit like ours and we would have stayed in the AMX 10 RC, well, we would’ve been wiped out a lot too, right? So you have to put things in perspective.”

During an exercise in March at Mailly-le-Camp, the 1er RIMa acted as the opposing force during the combat preparation of another French light-cavalry regiment. The drone squadron’s pilots attacked and virtually destroyed enemy armor, or would track vehicles to command posts or troop concentrations before striking, paralyzing enemy movement.

The result was “severe,” said FPV pilot Igor, who participated in the exercise. The opposing force was neutralized, at least virtually.

For the Army, the takeaway was that it wanted to equip five more regiments with an all-drone squadron, to be operational in 2027. With the Army accelerating its drone effort, the six combat-drone units will be operational by the year, said Gen. Bruno Baratz, head of Future Combat Command, at an innovation day organized by France’s 6th Armored Brigade in September.

The brigade, which was assigned to set up two of the five drone squadrons, decided to create a third drone-combat unit using its own resources, 6th Armored Brigade commander Gen. Maxime do Tran said at the innovation day. One of the brigade’s infantry regiments is now deployed to Romania with a drone-combat unit.

“The trial conducted by the 1st Marine Infantry Regiment demonstrated the value of this capability,” Do Tran said. “We didn’t wait.”

With the drone-combat squadron of the 1er RIMa “more of a cavalry, reconnaissance” unit, Do Tran says the 6th Armored Brigade has taken a slightly different approach for its infantry regiments, ensuring that drone pilots previously trained on the Akeron antitank missile or 81-millimeter mortars retain those skills through dual training.

He said the brigade staff realized that whenever the weather was unfavorable for drone operations, those dual capabilities were needed to retain lethality. Creating drone units also created a problem of mass, according to Do Tran.

During the brigade’s first combat-preparation exercises with combat-drones, commanders found that even if their drone pilots achieved initial saturation, a numerically strong enemy would still break through lines that lacked ground troops to stop a large force.

“We quickly get overwhelmed if we don’t also have men behind them with rifles or other weapons,” Do Tran said. “We need to maintain a certain balance between human mass and drone pilots.”

Combat-drone units will have different uses based on the regiment they’re part of, according to the general.

He said that for a light cavalry regiment such as the 1st RIMa, drones contribute to reconnaissance and intelligence gathering, while for an infantry regiment, the primary role will be delivering lethal firepower. For a regiment of Leclerc main battle tanks, the combat drone unit’s primary role is to protect the armored column, Do Tran said.

“It’s an entirely new set of rules to learn, an entirely new combination of capabilities to reinvent,” said Baratz. “The Army’s task today is to reinvent itself to be effective in this modern combat.”

Drone vulnerabilities

“We shouldn’t think that drones will make the old systems obsolete. Drones do have vulnerabilities: weather conditions, electronic jamming, other counter-drone systems being put in place. We still need our older systems for deep-strike operations, close-contact maneuvers and direct engagement.”

Officers of the 1er RIMa cautioned against over-interpreting the March exercise, with Maj. Sylvain saying the opposing regiment lacked adequate anti-drone capabilities and had few drones of its own, making an effective response difficult. Caussin likewise noted that during Orion, the exercise in free terrain limited realistic use of jamming by the opposing force.

Equipment used by the French Army’s 1st Marine Infantry Regiment is on display during an exercise at the Mailly-le-Camp training grounds on June 11, 2026. (Rudy Ruitenberg/staff)

“If we’re back in a tank tomorrow and opposite of us we have another drone unit, it’s going to be tough for us as well,” Staff Sgt. Tristan said. “So we need all this feedback to figure out how to fight back against that.”

For its own combat preparation in June, the 1er RIMa asked the Centac combined-arms training center that runs the exercises at Mailly-le-Camp to step up the challenge, particularly by creating a contested electronic warfare environment.

The training center also added 12-gauge shotguns to the infantry kit and emphasized camouflage. Soldiers unfolded camouflaged umbrellas bought at sporting goods store Decathlon whenever they were on the move, seeking to obscure their visual signature.

The umbrellas slowed the 1er RIMa’s drone operators, forcing them to spend more time identifying targets to avoid confusion with the numerous deer or boars at Mailly-le-Camp, said Lt. Col. Guillaume, head of training at the 1st Light Infantry Battalion that staffs the center.

The opposing force also remained more dispersed, avoided grouped movement and kept vehicles farther back, bringing them forward only when the situation was considered sufficiently safe. The transparency provided by drones clearly changed how the exercise unfolded and affected the conduct of operations, Lt. Col. Guillaume said.

While the drone advantage was less pronounced in June than in March, the 1er RIMa’s drone squadron obtained the best score of the roughly 30 Army units that passed through the Mailly-le-Camp training center over a 12-month period, Lt. Col. Guillaume said. The June exercise provided a better assessment of how the drone squadron fares against a more capable adversary, according to Caussin.

The regiment explored what might happen if the tactical deadlock observed in Ukraine were broken and maneuver is restored, with Caussin saying the drone squadron’s mobility would allow it to move rapidly between different parts of the battlefield, using drones to understand the tactical situation and fill gaps.

But the exercise also showed the battlefield overview provided by drones, while necessary to win an engagement, is not enough by itself. The speed at which intelligence can be converted into action is as important as the number of drones in the air, and traditional infantry elements remained necessary to provide direct kinetic action, Lt. Col. Guillaume said.

Jam detection

Electronic warfare reduced drone effectiveness on several occasions, allowing the opposing force to bring in armor to create a temporary advantage and secure objectives, said Lt. Col. Guillaume, describing a constant “game of cat and mouse.”

Because of the energy requirements of electronic warfare, jamming is not necessarily continuous. The regiment learned it needed to become better at determining when it was actually being jammed by the adversary, and when a failed maneuver was due to its own poor execution, according to Caussin.

For the regimental commander, the exercise confirmed the drone squadron complements rather than replaces armored formations, an additional layer to combined-arms warfare than a revolution. With the opponent better prepared for the drone threat, the June exercise forced the 1er RIMa to change how it employed drones, picking priority targets rather than observing and striking at will.

In a drone-saturated environment, advantage will come more from controlling the pace of events rather than simply holding ground, by combining drones and highly mobile forces to observe, adapt, and act faster than the adversary, according to Caussin.

He called the stand-out quality of the drone-combat unit its ability “manipulate the enemy’s decision-making time, really to overwhelm them” and allow the regiment to seize the initiative.

“Will the drone’s enemy be another drone?” Caussin said. “We’re starting to implement this to some extend in small-scale exercises, particularly at the regimental level.”

“It’s always difficult to talk about game-changers. What’s certain today is that this is the reality of the battlefield and it enables us to achieve tactical effects that have a decisive impact on the enemy.”

In the 1er RIMa, FVP pilots are teamed with a driver, a vehicle commander and optionally a fourth crew member operating a drone by Paris-based vendor Parrot, or other ISR system to designate targets. Cpl. Igor said he often works with one crew member as a spotter. The teams still carry Heckler & Koch rifles, a heavy machine gun to defend against enemy infantry, and an AT4 shoulder-fired antitank weapon.

Farther reach

With the 105 mm gun of the AMX 10 RC, “we had reach, but we were much more visible,” Staff Sgt. Tristan said. Now, “we hole up in a corner with our vehicle and launch our drones from hiding, so the enemy can’t see us. With an AMX 10 RC, we can’t do that.”

The regiment’s drone squadron retained the structure of an armored reconnaissance unit, with a command and logistics platoon and four combat platoons: an ISR unit, two FPV drone units and one platoon operating longer-range loitering munitions.

With around 100 drone pilots in a unit of 120 personnel, the squadron can provide intelligence and strike across an area typically covered by a formation several times larger, with an operational reach approaching that of a full combined-arms tactical group, according to Lt. Col. Remi.

A team from the French Army’s 1st Marine Infantry Regiment operates an FPV drone during an exercise at the Mailly-le-Camp training grounds on June 11, 2026. (Rudy Ruitenberg/staff)

The large number of drone operators changes logistics. Supply vehicles carry drones rather than ammunition, and the unit needs battlefield energy generation to keep batteries topped up. FPV pilot Igor said that during a day of exercise and simulated strikes, he might go through 10 or 20 drones.

Both Caussin and Maj. Sylvain emphasized drones alone will not decide a battle. The squadron is designed to operate alongside conventional units and slot into larger formations, with interoperability with French Army units and NATO allies built in from the start, said Maj. Sylvain.

In addition to developing doctrine, the regiment’s mandate is to work with industrial partners on technologies to make the drone squadron more effective. Once concept, described by officers and pilots as “queen and workers,” involves an operator controlling a mother drone, or queen, which in turn steers multiple worker drones launched from a “hive,” or canister.

The goal is essentially fire-and-forget autonomous engagement, according to Maj. Sylvain. An operator assigns a mission, the queen autonomously seeks and identifies the designated target, then directs worker drones to destroy it without further operator intervention.

With the squadron conceived as a mobile force rather than one dug in like Ukrainian operators, the idea is to have four hives at the back of a vehicle, with a stock of drones ready to deploy, Staff Sgt. Tristan said.

“Just open the hives, send out four drones on autopilot, and let them carry out their mission.”

While “not everything works perfect,” the squadron and its industry partners have figured out queen-worker communications and terminal guidance, Maj. Sylvain said.

FPV drone hit rates might be 5% to 10% for a novice pilot and 30% for an experienced one, compared with a more than 90% chance to land a shot with the AMX 10 RC cannon, according to Staff Sgt. Tristan. The regiment is counting on autonomous terminal guidance to increase drone hit rates to around 80% to 90%.

The squadron’s doctrinal goals include FPV strikes at a distance of 10 kilometers, and communication range is the main practical obstacle, Maj. Sylvain said. The ISR platoon uses several drone models from Parrot and is starting to achieve the ranges of up to 20 kilometers called for in the doctrine.

Officers in the 1er RIMa shied away from comparisons with Ukrainian drone units, which Maj. Sylvain said are battle-hardened after several years of war.

‘We’re not naive’

“It’s obvious that we’d learn a lot from an exercise against them,” he said. “We’re not naive. We’re fully aware of our current level of capability, and we know we still have a lot of work to do to be ready to deploy the drone-combat squadron in a high-intensity conflict.”

The June exercise was also an opportunity to work on defense against enemy UAVs.

“Camouflage ourselves, take cover, and make sure there’s always someone listening out for drones, either to destroy them with certain weapons or simply to hide,” Staff Sgt. Tristan said. “In the heat of battle, chasing down a vehicle, also staying alert for anti-drone is difficult.”

The constant drone threat changes daily life for soldiers on exercise. Rather than having fixed meal times, “you have to snack throughout the day,” said Staff Sgt. Tristan. Sleep also becomes fragmented, with drone surveillance continuing through the night.

The 1er RIMa itself has adapted its command structure to the drone presence, dividing up command posts and deploying in the same armored vehicles as combat units to avoid detection.

“We question ourselves, we wonder how we can protect ourselves from this too,” said Cpl. Igor, the FPV pilot. “If we’re capable of doing it, we’re not the only ones who know how. So we’re going to try to find ways to protect ourselves from drones, because they’re really effective.”

A GJ-21 stealthy flying-wing uncrewed combat air vehicle (UCAV) has successfully launched from and recovered aboard the Type 076 amphibious assault ship Sichuan, according to China’s People’s Liberation Army (PLA). Visual confirmation of this milestone has yet to emerge, but there have been steady signs of preparations for flight trials from the Sichuan, which features an electromagnetic catapult. GJ-21s have already been spotted flying from airfields on land.

A picture of a GJ-21 drone that previously emerged online. Chinese internet

The GJ-21 and the land-based GJ-11 Mysterious Dragon it is based on are prime examples of China’s major investments in UCAVs and other flying-wing-type drones. These developments stand in particular contrast to the U.S. military’s eschewing of such designs, at least publicly. This is despite significant past U.S. achievements in this arena, including in the development of carrier-based UCAVs. The PLA Navy (PLAN) now looks to be on track to be the first country to field an operational capability of this kind.

A stock picture of the Type 076 amphibious assault ship Sichuan. PLA

“China’s first ship-borne stealth reconnaissance and attack drone ‘GJ-21’ recently made its first successful take-off and landing on [the Type] 076 amphibious assault ship, the ‘Sichuan’ (Hull 51),” according to a post today from the China Military Bugle account on X, an official PLA mouthpiece. “This is another significant breakthrough in the development of China’s amphibious assault ship, indicating that the Chinese Navy now has the capability to operate fixed-wing stealth drones from large maritime combat platforms.”

At the time of writing, neither the PLA nor Chinese state media have shared where or when these trials occurred or provided any other additional details, including how many total launches and recoveries were conducted. No official pictures or videos from the testing have been released so far.

A graphic that accompanied the post on the China Military Bugle’s account on X regarding the GJ-21 and the Type 076. PLA/China Military Bugle

Unconfirmed claims that a GJ-21 had been launched from Sichuan had already begun circulating on social media earlier this month. As an aside, the GJ-21 is readily discernible from the land-based GJ-11 by the catapult bar on its nose landing gear strut.

The GJ-21 will of course not be the only aircraft in the Type 076’s future air wing. The ship is also expected to carry a variety of other uncrewed aircraft, as well as crewed helicopters. China has shown a range of other combat drone designs in recent years. Navalized versions could well be developed for deployment on Sichuan, as well as PLAN’s growing fleet of traditional carriers and other suitable configured vessels.

Loading the Type 076 with operational UCAVs would only underscore the significance of this particular ship. As we have previously written:

“The potential value to the PLAN of having a fleet of very large deck amphibious assault ships that are highly capable of large-scale drone operations as well as traditional amphibious assaults is clear. These ships could be used to launch and recover UCAVs like the GJ-11 and other types of drones to perform a host of missions from maritime strike to intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance (ISR). They could be used to provide additional ‘mass’ in support of the operations of larger carrier strike groups and help to free the air wings of flattops like Fujian up for tasks they might be better suited for. In addition, they could provide a lower tier of naval aviation support that could be employed independently.”

Type 076s could also help project naval and air power further from China’s shores, something that has also been a driving factor behind the country’s broader carrier ambitions.”

“The Type 076 could help provide support during humanitarian assistance and disaster relief operations. Big deck amphibious warfare ships, in general, provide their operators with immensely valuable platforms for projecting both hard and soft power.”

Last year, the PLA separately confirmed that the GJ-11 had entered at least limited operational service. That followed a steady ramp-up in testing of that drone based on what could be seen in satellite imagery and video clips that had emerged online. This included evidence of near-daily flights at Malan Air Base, a major hub for testing uncrewed platforms. GJ-11s had also been spotted at an operational air base in China’s Tibet Autonomous Region.

As mentioned earlier, the continued development of the GJ-11 and GJ-21 are reflective of the PLA’s broader pursuit of stealthy flying-wing drones, which is not limited to UCAVs. Several larger designs that look most immediately suited to high-altitude, long-endurance ISR missions have also broken cover in the country in the past few years.

There is something of a trend in this regard globally, with Russia, India, Turkey, and France also pursuing flying-wing drone designs. The U.S. military continues to be markedly absent from this space, at least that we know, despite major investments and real demonstrations of relevant capability, before various programs were abandoned for reasons that still remain largely unclear. In the late 2010s, the U.S. Navy notably looked to be right on the edge of fielding a carrier-based UCAV, before changing course entirely to an uncrewed tanker instead. There are some signs now that the service may be taking another look at the capabilities it passed over.

It is still to be seen how quickly the PLA’s official statement today will be followed by real confirmation of the launching and recovery of a GJ-21 from the Sichuan.
