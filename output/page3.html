Highlights From North Korea’s Drone-A-Palooza

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

North Korean leader Kim Jong Un presided over a major exhibition of uncrewed aerial vehicles (UAVs) on Thursday. A mockup of an apparent new large stealth drone was displayed prominently alongside an increasingly diverse collection of reconnaissance drones, armed UAVs, loitering munitions, and small attack drones. While there is no evidence that North Korea is capable of getting a stealthy flying-wing drone flying, let alone operating as a operationally relevant low-observable platform, there are no doubts that the country has an increasingly impressive uncrewed aircraft arsenal, with the smaller, lower-end types being of greatest importance.

An overview of the Unmanned Aeronautical Technology Development-2026 exhibition, with the “stealth drone” in pride of place. KCNA

As for the “stealth drone,” photographs released by North Korean state media show a mockup of a tailless flying-wing design. In terms of appearance, there are clear parallels with advanced uncrewed combat aerial vehicles (UCAVs) developed by the United States, China, and Russia.

However, huge questions remain as to what degree the mockup is part of a realistic program. We have no details of the program’s development status and planned capabilities. It is highly questionable if Pyongyang is able to produce a functional prototype. Even if it can get it into the air, there is no evidence that it possesses the capabilities to master meaningful stealth technologies, let alone the command-and-control functions and other elements needed for it to operate in a tactically relevant manner.

The exhibition, titled Unmanned Aeronautical Technology Development-2026, was held on Thursday, with images published by the ruling Workers’ Party newspaper Rodong Sinmun today. Kim called for the country to achieve “absolute superiority” in drone technology and announced unspecified measures to accelerate the development of uncrewed aviation systems and military artificial intelligence.

Kim Jong Un walks past rows of quadcopter drones at the exhibition. Rodong Sinmun

An Ambitious New Flying-Wing Design

The mockup of the new aircraft was positioned prominently in the exhibition hall, suggesting that Pyongyang considers it at least indicative of its broader ambitions.

Based on the available photographs, Hong Min, a senior research fellow at the Korea Institute for National Unification, interviewed by NK News, estimated the wingspan at roughly 56 to 66 feet and its height at around 10 to 11.5 feet. Standout features include a dorsal air intake, a relatively large center fuselage section integrated into the flying wing, and retractable tricycle landing gear.

Another view of North Korea’s apparent new stealth drone. KCTV

In terms of general arrangement, there are similarities to Northrop Grumman’s X-47B, the U.S. Navy’s experimental carrier-based UCAV. There are also visual similarities with China’s GJ-11 Sharp Sword and Russia’s S-70 Okhotnik, two other large flying-wing UCAV designs.

X-47B during carrier operations. U.S. Navy GJ-11 Sharp Sword UCAV. via Chinese internet

At this point, North Korea has not provided any technical information about the aircraft’s propulsion system, payload, intended mission, or flight performance.

Critically, while the external shape is consistent with a stealthy UCAV, a flying-wing configuration on its own is only one part of a far more complex puzzle in achieving a genuinely low radar signature. Other factors include the engine inlet and exhaust arrangements, radar-absorbing materials, surface treatments, manufacturing tolerances, and the alignment of individual edges and surfaces, low probability of intercept communications and much more.

Officials inspect the new flying-wing UCAV design. Rodong Sinmun

The Iranian RQ-170 Precedent

Overall, there are many good reasons to be extremely cautious about North Korea’s apparent ambitions in this area.

Iran is another country that has displayed a drone that resembles an advanced Western stealth platform, without actually reproducing its capabilities. It has, however, gotten a UCAV-lookalike into the air.

After recovering a U.S. RQ-170 Sentinel reconnaissance drone that came down inside Iranian territory in 2011, Tehran announced that it had successfully reverse-engineered the aircraft. In 2014, Iranian authorities displayed what they presented as a domestically produced copy of the American drone, claiming that it would soon undertake a test flight.

The actual RQ-170 Sentinel Iran recovered on display. Iranian State Media

Outside observers questioned whether the displayed aircraft was anything more than a non-functional replica, noting apparent shortcomings in its construction and the use of components inconsistent with a sophisticated operational aircraft.

Iran subsequently presented a family of aircraft derived from the RQ-170 concept, including the Shahed-171 Simorgh and the smaller Shahed-191. Iran has demonstrated flying examples of these designs, but they fall far short of replicating the RQ-170’s actual low-observable qualities, let alone its sensors and avionics.

Iran’s self-proclaimed RQ-170-like long-range UCAVs. AP

In the case of Pyongyang, however, the possibility of Russian assistance is at least worth considering.

North Korea’s burgeoning military relationship with Moscow, including the deployment of North Korean troops to support Russia’s war in Ukraine, could provide a chance for the exchange of technical knowledge and operational experience from Moscow. Russia has developed and flown the S-70 Okhotnik UCAV, exposing it to some operational trials in Ukraine, and could have passed related information on to Pyongyang.

An S-70 taxiing after landing. Russian Ministry of Defense

In particular, North Korea would welcome Russian assistance in developing and manufacturing propulsion, flight control, sensor, and materials technologies.

However, there is so far no proof of technology transfer from Russia. Even if it were to be provided, it is far from clear that this would suffice to get a true UCAV into the air anytime soon, although an Iran-style knockoff with far reduced capabilities cannot be ruled out entirely. It’s also worth noting that Russia’s own UCAV program has been anything but a smooth and rapid advancement to an operational state.

Overall, the new flying-wing concept does not represent a realistic technological undertaking — at least for now. Very few countries have managed to develop and fly drones in this class, and fewer still have been able to field them operationally.

North Korea’s Drone Development Effort

The flying-wing aircraft was only one part of a much larger display. More than 30 types of UAVs reportedly appeared at the exhibition, reflecting North Korea’s efforts to diversify its uncrewed aviation capabilities.

Some of these designs had been seen before, including the Saetbyol-4 and Saetbyol-9. These were first displayed in 2023 and are, in terms of appearance, clones of the U.S. RQ-4 Global Hawk and MQ-9 Reaper, respectively.

Kim Jong Un inspects an armed Saetbyol-9. Rodong Sinmun

Imagery released of the Saetbyol-9 showed it carrying — and firing — several types of air-to-ground weapons. These include a Hellfire-like laser-guided missile, along with smaller rockets and other possible guided munitions.

Saetbyol-9 fires a rocket or missile. Rodong Sinmun

Other exhibits included loitering munitions resembling Israel’s Harop and Russia’s Lancet, as well as smaller drones with configurations similar to the U.S.-made Switchblade family.

Kim Jong Un inspecting Switchblade-style loitering munitions. Rodong Sinmun

Hundreds of small quadcopter drones were also displayed on storage racks. Some appeared to carry cylindrical containers associated with fiber-optic control systems, a technology that has become increasingly prominent in the Russia-Ukraine war. Unlike conventional radio-controlled drones, fiber-optic systems are largely immune to conventional radio-frequency jamming of their control links. These drones would be a major feature in a cross-DMZ conflict.

North Korean interest in artificial intelligence was another recurring theme. Kim called for the development of increasingly intelligent uncrewed weapons, although there is no indication of how advanced the country’s existing autonomous navigation, target recognition, or engagement systems actually are.

The exhibition was accompanied by live-fire demonstrations involving UAVs, cruise missiles, and multiple rocket launchers. North Korean state media claimed that missiles launched from drones and other systems struck an island target “with 100 percent accuracy.”

A barrage of rockets fired by a large-caliber multiple rocket launcher. Rodong Sinmun

From Ambition To Operational Capabilities

North Korea’s rapidly expanding interest in drones should also be seen against the shortcomings of its aging crewed combat aviation fleet. Relatively inexpensive UAVs offer opportunities for reconnaissance and attacks against military infrastructure without exposing pilots to enemy air defenses. At the same time, Pyongyang has failed to introduce new crewed combat aircraft to modernize its air force, the capabilities of which are thoroughly outmatched by those of South Korea.

The war in Ukraine (as well as lessons from conflicts in the Middle East) also provides Pyongyang with an opportunity to study the use of drones on a large scale. North Korean troops deployed to Russia will have gained first-hand experience of drone warfare, including the integration of reconnaissance UAVs, FPV attack drones, and electronic warfare systems.

That experience could help accelerate the development of North Korea’s own uncrewed platforms, particularly relatively simple systems that can be produced in large quantities.

An attack drone in the Shahed/Geran class, for example, could be produced relatively easily in very large numbers (and that is likely already happening to some degree) and could saturate South Korean air defenses, which are generally tailored to address more conventional threats. North Korea doesn’t need the long range that the Shahed/Geran typically provides, since many of its primary targets are much closer. The shorter distances involved could permit a greater proportion of a drone’s weight to be allocated to its warhead, depending on the design. Alternatively, a drone with the roughly 1,000-mile reach of the Shahed/Geran could fly a very complex route to reach targets in the South, including approaching via the coast. At the very least, expanding North Korean efforts in this area could tilt the balance of power on the peninsula, providing Seoul with a major interdiction problem and forcing the development of new countermeasures, such as directed-energy weapons and cheap interceptors.

A typical Russian Shahed-136/Geran-2-series kamikaze drone over Ukraine. via X

The possibility of Russian assistance via its own Geran development effort is likely bringing enhanced technologies within reach of North Korea. Indeed, that kind of expertise is already being gained by tens of thousands of North Korean workers who have been sent to Russia as additional labor for its drone-production efforts.

A look inside a Russian factory where licensed production of Iranian Shahed-series one-way attack drones is taking place. via X

And once again, it’s these one-way attack munitions and smaller drones that can be mass-produced and rapidly iterated upon that pose a real threat to South Korea. It is likely that North Korea will continue to ramp-up its investment in these systems, building them in very large tranches in order to create an asymmetric advantage — one that has proven challenging to counter in real conflicts around the globe. At the same time, they will also continue to build high-tech-looking clones of far more advanced systems which will have little tactical or strategic impact but work well for propaganda purposes.

From tanks to space, Norway and Germany forge ahead with ‘Hansa’ defense pact

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

VIENNA — German tank maker KNDS and Norway’s Ritek have begun producing Leopard 2A8 tanks in Norway, adding a land-based industrial foothold to a security partnership spanning submarines, missiles and military operations in Europe’s north.

Production started Oct. 15 in Levanger, where the companies will build 37 of the Norwegian Army’s 54 new tanks, KNDS said in an email. The facility took 18 months to establish and has a stated capacity of up to 36 tanks annually.

The production launch follows the partners’ inauguration of the manufacturing and testing infrastructure in late April. October’s milestone caps an existing procurement arrangement rather than marking a new tank purchase.

The industrial tie-up forms part of a broader mutual embrace that the two governments formalized in February through the sweeping Hansa Arrangement. Its priorities include maritime operations, combined-arms land warfare, rapid reinforcement, defense industry, and space-based surveillance, targeting and communications, according to a readout by Norway’s Defense Ministry.

An ongoing submarine partnership offers the clearest example of how equipment purchases can bind the countries’ armed forces and security strategies together. Both plan to operate six identical Type 212CD submarines, supported by cooperation on personnel training and new maintenance facilities at Haakonsvern, near Bergen, the Norwegian ministry said. Berlin and Oslo are also collaborating on the next-generation 3SM maritime strike missile.

Although industry cooperation forms the bedrock, the relationship is moving beyond mere procurement questions. Germany contributed approximately 1,800 troops to Norway’s Cold Response exercise in March, according to the German government. Norway said in February that discussions were underway about German equipment pre-positioning on its territory.

Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine in early 2022 accelerated a longstanding partnership, researchers at the Norwegian Institute of International Affairs concluded in a December 2024 assessment. Germany’s growing dependence on Norwegian gas also strengthened its interest in protecting offshore infrastructure, researcher Øyvind Svendsen explained.

Germany’s military is undergoing a distinct shift toward a bigger role in the North Atlantic. Its new P-8A Poseidon aircraft, for example, are covering the so-called GIUK gap between Greenland, Iceland and the UK, a crucial area that Russian nuclear submarines must pass through to reach the open Atlantic. German Defense Minister Boris Pistorius said the aircraft would operate from Norwegian and Icelandic air bases for this mission, Defense News previously reported. 

German Chancellor Friedrich Merz and Jonas Gahr Støre, Prime Minister of Norway, sit next to each other at the Andøya Space Visitor Center during the “Round Table on Space Cooperation” in Andenes, Norway, on Feb. 20, 2026. (Soeren Stache/picture alliance via Getty Images)

Space cooperation offers another avenue for growing collaboration. During a March visit to Andøya, Pistorius identified a joint imaging-reconnaissance satellite constellation as a project he wanted to pursue, according to his ministry. 

In April, Oslo and Berlin agreed to create a bilateral space working group, with priorities including launch capabilities, space-based surveillance and secure communications. Norway is home to a valuable spaceport on the island of Andøya, which is particularly well situated for launching satellites into polar orbits, which are preferable for spy satellites. 

The two North Sea countries are also cooperating in a number of other formats, including under the Multinational Multi Role Tanker Transport Fleet, which pools access to Airbus A330 MRTT aircraft for aerial refueling, strategic transport and medical evacuation, and EU HYDEF, where Germany and Norway are among five co-funders working on phase one of a European hypersonic interceptor missile.

Ukraine completes work on new missile defense system, awaits European partner components

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

KYIV, Ukraine — Ukraine has completed its share of Freyja, the multinational antiballistic shield that Europe and Kyiv are racing to bring together, the country’s president, Volodymyr Zelenskyy, said earlier this month, putting the onus on partner countries and their defense contractors to develop the interceptor’s remaining components fast enough to conduct initial tests by year’s end. 

Fire Point, the Ukrainian company leading the project, is building the FP-7.X interceptor and its launcher while partners such as Norway’s Kongsberg and Germany’s Diehl Defence are responsible for developing parts that find, track and guide the missile to its target.

“Ukraine has done what it was supposed to do, and that is good news,” Zelenskyy told journalists Oct. 17. “As for what the partners are supposed to do, you know that there are ten participating countries, and they are in the process.”

The push comes as Ukraine runs short of costly Patriot interceptors and Russia fields faster weapons, including jet-powered Shahed drones, forcing Kyiv to spend scarce antiballistics on both drones and increasingly large missile barrages. 

Russia has fired more than 100 ballistic missiles at Kyiv and the surrounding region in the past two weeks after launching nearly 3,500 jet-powered drones across Ukraine in September, Zelenskyy said, quoting air force figures. 

The Coalition of the Willing Summit on July 13, 2026, Paris, France. (X)

Civilian casualties from January through September ran 59% higher than a year earlier, according to the U.N.

“People work on systems like this for decades. We want it very fast, because there is no time,” Zelenskyy said about the new antiballistic system.

Freyja is designed to end Kyiv’s dependence on the Patriot and provide Europe with its own antiballistic capability. However, now that Ukraine has delivered the system’s base elements, one of the program’s most demanding phases – fitting European sensors, seekers and fire-control systems to a Ukrainian interceptor – hinges on partners’ pace of development. 

Fire Point had already begun “the most intense and also the most complicated stage” of final assembly with specially designed radars and seekers from other countries, capping an overall effort to “squeeze a [antiballistics development] program that usually takes 20 or 30 years into a matter of a couple of years,” chief executive Iryna Terekh said in August. 

Ukrainian officials consider the Patriot their only reliable defense against ballistic missiles, but the U.S.-Israel war with Iran has drained an estimated two-thirds of America’s prewar PAC-3 interceptor stock.

The missiles are also expensive and slow to arrive. A PAC-3 interceptor costs roughly $3.8 million, takes years to deliver, and can get held up in licensing talks or delivery issues.

Fire Point unveiled Freyja in May to end that dependence, offering Kyiv and its allies an interceptor they can build and deploy independently.

Ukraine and nine European partners, including France, Germany and the United Kingdom, officially launched Freyja’s coalition in Paris on July 13.

“Europeans themselves will be able to decide how many systems Europe needs and where they should be deployed,” Zelenskyy said at the launch. Poland joined the cohort in September, and Canada is “ready to contribute its technologies,” Zelenskyy said after meeting Prime Minister Mark Carney Sept. 10, according to the presidential office.

Under a June agreement with Germany’s Hensoldt, Fire Point is the prime contractor and design authority, and Hensoldt supplies the radars, such as the TRML-4D, which the company says can track roughly 1,500 air targets.

A preliminary design shared by Fire Point co-founder Denys Shtilerman on X in May pairs the FP-7.X with a long-range radar from Sweden’s Saab, France’s Thales or Hensoldt, a fire-control radar from Denmark’s Weibel or Italy’s Leonardo, and a command center from Kongsberg, maker of the NASAMS systems Ukraine already uses.

The system would tie into Ukraine’s wider air-defense network over NATO’s Link 16 standard, connect its radars through the Asterix protocol and use a two-way data link to correct the interceptor’s course in flight, according to the slides. 

European partners will supply that data link and the command center, Reuters reported in June. Most of the system’s components are designed to be interchangeable.

The system’s seeker remains the bottleneck, Terekh told The War Zone, adding that Fire Point is integrating Diehl’s infrared seeker and developing other options with two unnamed partners.

“If we look at everything but the seeker, our production is already ready, even for mass production,” she said. “Even the seeker manufacturers are doing this for the first time in such an integration.”

The first FP-7.X will use a blast-fragmentation warhead, closer to the older PAC-2 than the hit-to-kill PAC-3, and intercept at roughly 12 to 16 miles (20 to 25 kilometers) up, Terekh told Janes in July. A hit-to-kill version will follow.

Fire Point aims to produce at least 2,000 interceptors a year for under $1.2 million (€1 million) each, though Terekh said that price would come “probably not from the first iterations, but definitely in serial production.”

Frejya’s next technical milestones depend on European partners.

“If, for example, every European government starts moving swiftly, we can do interceptors by the end of this year,” Fire Point’s Shtilerman said. The company is aiming for battlefield use next July.

In Oslo last week, Zelenskyy, Norwegian Defense Minister Tore Sandvik and Kongsberg chief executive Eirik Lie identified where work “can and must be accelerated to begin testing by the end of this year,” Ukraine’s presidential office reported.

“Fortunately for Europe and for Ukraine itself, we have Fire Point,” EU Defense and Space Commissioner Andrius Kubilius said Oct. 17, according to UNN, adding that its work on ballistic missiles and Freyja “could become a huge change.”

Iran Used Limpet Mine Placed On Ship’s Hull In Latest Strait Of Hormuz Attack: Report

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A small boat in the Strait of Hormuz approached a tanker and placed two explosive devices on the hull above the waterline, causing a blast that started a fire in the engine room, the United Kingdom Maritime Trade Operations (UKMTO) center reported. The attack on the Dubai-owned tanker Louise 1 was carried out by Iranians using limpet mines, Financial Times reported. The incident took place Thursday and was the first of its kind since June 2019, when a Japanese and a Norwegian ship were hit with limpets, the publication noted.

This is the first indication of the successful use of these mines during the current Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps’ (IRGC) wave of strikes against shipping in the Strait of Hormuz following Operation Epic Fury. The attack also highlights the challenges of keeping ships safe in a busy and complex littoral.

“The apparent ability of a small craft to approach a transiting tanker, deploy explosive devices and withdraw presents a significant security concern, particularly in the Strait’s congested shipping lanes,” Greece-based maritime ​risk management group Marisks said ​on ⁠Friday, Reuters reported.

TWZ covered the 2019 attacks extensively, including sharing the following video showing an Iranian Navy or IRGC fast boat going back to remove a dud limpet mine from the Japanese-owned Kokuka Courageous’s damaged hull.

Earlier Friday morning, U.S. Central Command (CENTCOM) commander Adm. Brad Cooper said the waterway was cleared of sea mines. He was not addressing the limpet mine claim.

He stated on X that after “we sank Iran’s navy earlier this year, we also successfully cleared the strait’s internationally recognized primary transit routes of Iranian sea mines, and today U.S. forces remain highly vigilant and present to ensure that all primary transit routes in the Strait of Hormuz remain free and clear.”

“This week we reached a significant milestone, having now assisted Gulf partners in exporting more than 1.5 billion barrels of crude oil since summer,” Cooper added.

Recalling a recent flight over the Strait of Hormuz, Cooper noted that he “saw our U.S. assets in the air and on the water, including a squadron of advanced sea drones that were on patrol supporting a defensive layer. Here’s what I didn’t see: I didn’t see the IRGC out there at all.”

Also known as uncrewed surface vessels (USVs), the sea drones are part of an increasingly uncrewed armada of air, land and sea assets that are supporting the safe corridor through the Strait.

The USV type shown in the following video, a Saronic Corsair, made history twice in the battle against Iran. In July, the USVs were used as a strike weapon in combat for the first time by the U.S. A month earlier, a Saronic Corsair USV rescued the crew of a U.S. Army AH-64 Apache that crashed in the Gulf of Oman after it was downed by Iran.

In a sign of the times, for the first time in many years, U.S. Air Force fighters have returned to an air defense alert posture at Eareckson Air Station in Alaska’s remote Aleutian Islands. This represents a notable development in efforts to strengthen NORAD’s ability to defend North America’s northern and Pacific approaches from a key strategic stronghold on Russia’s back doorstep. The deployment culminated in a multinational air defense mission involving U.S. and Canadian fighters operating across thousands of miles, reflecting a growing focus on the increasingly strategic Arctic and North Pacific regions.

A fully armed U.S. Air Force F-16 taxis at Eareckson Air Station, Alaska, during Operation Noble Defender, Oct. 18, 2026. Noble Defender is a recurring NORAD air defense operation that demonstrates the command’s ability to defend the United States and Canada against threats from every approach. U.S. Air Force photo by Airman 1st Class Frank Houseton

Fighter operations at Eareckson date back to the Cold War, when F-4s and F-15s deployed to the island to support air defense missions against Soviet aircraft. The base’s routine fighter mission ended in the early 1990s, making the October 2026 deployment a notable return to an alert posture at one of America’s westernmost military outposts. In the interim, we have seen fighters, including F-16s, return to Eareckson, but not in the context of air defense alert missions.

Packing live air-to-air missiles, the F-16s were deployed to Eareckson, on Shemya Island, from October 18 through 26, maintaining an alert posture before launching on October 25 as part of a homeland defense mission.

A satellite image of Shemya Island. Eareckson Air Station, with its lengthy runway, is seen along the southern edge. Google Earth

The latest mission began under Operation Noble Defender before transitioning into Operation Noble Eagle in support of northern sovereignty operations. It brought together F-16s operating from Eareckson, F-35As and additional F-16s from Eielson Air Force Base, and Royal Canadian Air Force CF-18 Hornets deployed from Cold Lake, Alberta.

The F-16s operating from Eielson were assigned to the 18th Fighter Interceptor Squadron (FIS), a particularly unusual unit with roots as the 18th Aggressor Squadron. The unit was redesignated in 2024, shifting its primary combat focus from adversary training toward aerospace control and homeland defense in Alaska. Its Block 30 F-16s bring a combination of air defense capabilities and experience in replicating adversary tactics, making them a very specialized outfit, as you can read about here.

From their distributed locations, the Eareckson-based F-16s provided coverage in the southern portion of the theater, while fighters from Eielson operated in the northwest. Canadian CF-18s covered the northern approaches.

U.S. Air Force F-16s assigned to Eielson Air Force Base sit parked on the flight line at Eareckson Air Station, Alaska, on Oct. 19, 2026. U.S. Air Force photo by Airman 1st Class Frank Houseton

An E-3 Sentry Airborne Warning and Control System (AWACS) aircraft from Joint Base Elmendorf-Richardson provided airborne command and control, supported by multiple U.S. KC-135 tankers and a Canadian CC-150 Polaris. An HC-130 Combat King II also participated, providing contingency search and rescue and personnel recovery coverage.

The distances involved illustrate the scale of the operation. Eareckson is approximately 1,600 miles from Eielson and 2,600 miles from Cold Lake, requiring substantial coordination and aerial refueling support to bring the different elements together.

A map showing just how remote Shemya Island is, as well as its relative position to mainland Alaska to the east and Russia’s Kamchatka Peninsula to the west. Google Earth

Strategic Significance

Located at the western end of the Aleutian chain, Shemya occupies a strategically important position in the North Pacific, facing the approaches from Russia’s Far East and the wider Pacific region and acting as a gateway to the Arctic. While fighters operating from Joint Base Elmendorf-Richardson, with additional support from Eielson when needed, normally provide coverage across this vast area, Eareckson puts combat aircraft dramatically farther west. Its reactivation as a fighter operating location gives NORAD another forward option, extending the reach of its air defense network well beyond Alaska’s principal airbases.

The island’s military significance was especially pronounced during the Cold War, when it supported detachments of RC-135 surveillance aircraft, including hosting Cobra Ball missile-tracking aircraft, as you can read about here.

An RC-135S Cobra Ball outside a hangar at Shemya. Robert S. Hopkins III

Shemya’s potential role in a future conflict has attracted increasing attention from U.S. military planners in recent years. The island hosts the AN/FPS-108 Cobra Dane radar, a massive phased-array installation that provides critical ballistic missile warning and tracking capabilities. Its position in the western Aleutians, roughly 500 miles from Russia’s Kamchatka Peninsula and approximately 1,000 miles from mainland Alaska, gives it considerable strategic importance but also leaves it isolated and potentially vulnerable.

The growing threat posed by one-way attack drones and cruise missiles also makes protecting Cobra Dane a significant force-protection challenge. Forward-deployed fighters provide a highly adaptable, wide-area defensive layer, intercepting aerial threats before they reach the island, much like the fighter deployments to Diego Garcia in the Indian Ocean.

The vulnerability of Shemya and Cobra Dane has been the focus of several military exercises. In 2021, Green Berets assigned to Special Operations Command North participated in a NORAD exercise that involved deploying to Shemya and preparing to defend the Cobra Dane installation against potential attack. The troops arrived by sea and brought Stinger man-portable air defense systems, highlighting concerns about protecting the island against both conventional and unconventional threats.

Special operators assigned to Special Operations Command North (SOCNORTH), including Army Green Berets from the 10th Special Forces Group, practice the defense of the AN/FPS-108 Cobra Dane early warning and tracking radar at Shemya. U.S. Air Force

In August 2023, elements of the Army’s 160th Special Operations Aviation Regiment, better known as the Night Stalkers, returned to the island as part of Operation Polar Dagger, as TWZ reported at the time. At least two MH-60M Black Hawk helicopters and associated personnel and equipment deployed to Eareckson, with a C-17 transporting personnel and materiel. The exercise explored the ability to rapidly project special operations capabilities into the remote region, protect critical infrastructure, and improve operational awareness in the Arctic and sub-Arctic.

The Cobra Dane radar installation is seen in the background of this picture of an MH-60M helicopter from the 160th SOAR on Shemya Island during the Polar Dagger exercise in 2023. U.S. Army

Taken together, these developments point to growing interest in Shemya as a potential forward operating location in a wider North Pacific defense network. Its radar, long runway, and remote position offer considerable advantages, but its isolation also presents significant logistical and force-protection challenges.

The return of F-16s to an air defense alert posture at Eareckson adds another dimension to the installation. With its ability to host forward-deployed fighters, the base can maintain an alert posture and generate homeland defense sorties. Its location would be particularly useful for responding to activity approaching Alaska from the western Pacific and the Bering Sea. At the same time, having fighters at Eareckson provides an organic means of defending Shemya’s critical surveillance assets.

A U.S. Air Force F-16 taxis at Eareckson Air Station, Alaska, on Oct. 18, 2026. U.S. Air Force Photo by Maj. David Brown

There are other potential benefits in dispersing fighter operations away from Alaska’s principal airbases. In a major conflict, those installations could be subject to missile strikes and other attacks, making alternative operating locations valuable. Eareckson’s long runway, ramp space, and arresting equipment provide a foundation for such operations, although sustaining fighters there over an extended period would require significant fuel, munitions, maintenance, and force-protection support.

Outside of Eareckson, Alaska has a network of remote airfields that can support dispersed fighter operations during contingencies. These include King Salmon Airport on the Alaska Peninsula, which hosted four 18th FIS F-16s during an Agile Combat Employment deployment in September last year.

All this comes as concerns grow about the level of protection afforded to Air Force airbases. There are growing calls for the U.S. military to invest in new hardened shelters, and the lack of physical hardening of bases has been hammered home by the experiences from the war with Iran, which have underscored the huge risks posed to aircraft sat out in the open on flightlines.

A U.S. Air Force KC-135 lands at Eareckson Air Station, Alaska, on Oct. 18, 2026. U.S. Air Force Photo by Maj. David Brown

The October deployment does not mean Eareckson is being transformed into a permanently staffed fighter base. It does, however, demonstrate that NORAD can use this isolated installation as another node in its air defense network, extending the geographic reach and flexibility of its operations across the North Pacific.

A U.S. Air Force maintainer performs preflight inspections on an F-16 at Eareckson Air Station, Alaska, on Oct. 18, 2026. U.S. Air Force Photo by Maj. David Brown

For NORAD, having additional locations from which it can generate air defense sorties is a major plus. The combination of forward-based fighters, airborne command and control, aerial refueling, and Canadian participation demonstrates another way of distributing combat power across a broad geographic area rather than depending exclusively on a handful of major bases.

More broadly, the Aleutians are assuming renewed strategic importance as the westernmost part of U.S. territory, positioned at the intersection of the North Pacific and Arctic theaters. As military competition with both Russia and China intensifies, the island chain offers a valuable forward position for surveillance, air defense, and power projection, as well as a means of gaining time and distance against potential threats approaching North America or operating across the Pacific. The return of F-16 alert operations to Eareckson adds another piece to that defensive architecture, underscoring the continued military relevance of this remote island chain.

Hasty US exit from Europe could drive allies away from American arms, RAND says

📅 Oct 29, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

A rapid, uncoordinated U.S. military drawdown from Europe could push key NATO allies further away from American weapons over the long term, according to a new RAND study, a shift the authors say would hurt the U.S. defense industrial base.

RAND released the report, “Anticipating Allies’ Responses to U.S. Retrenchment from Europe,” on Wednesday. It examines how France, Germany, Poland and the U.K. would likely respond to different versions of what RAND calls limited retrenchment: cuts to American commitments and forces in Europe that stop short of leaving NATO altogether.

The study draws on Cold War precedents, allied government documents and public debate, and interviews with 51 officials and experts in the four countries, most conducted between April and November 2025.

The authors caution that their findings apply only to those four allies.

The report’s main finding is that how the U.S. withdraws will shape how allies respond. The four allies “see a stark difference between a coordinated, orderly U.S. withdrawal, which they feel they could handle, and a rapid, uncoordinated one, which they see as creating major capability gaps and potentially emboldening Russia to attack a NATO member,” the authors wrote.

The authors recommend the U.S. follow through with larger force cuts on a long-term timeline worked out with allies, which they say may help keep allies’ defense spending on track even as it gets harder to sell at home.

RAND weighs those choices against NATO 3.0, the alliance concept Under Secretary of Defense for Policy Elbridge Colby presented in February. It calls for Europe to take primary responsibility for its own non-nuclear defense while the U.S. shifts resources to the Western Hemisphere and the Pacific.

The Pentagon has already begun reducing its footprint in Europe. About 80,000 U.S. troops were stationed there across roughly 30 bases as of 2025, according to congressional testimony from the head of U.S. European Command cited in the report.

In May, the Pentagon announced it would pull about 5,000 troops out of Germany. Then in June, the U.S. cut the forces it would make available to NATO in a crisis. A review of U.S. forces in Europe is underway.

Defense ministers from France, Germany, Italy, Poland and the U.K. said Monday in a joint statement that Europe “must assume greater responsibility for its own security.” They also called the U.S. “an indispensable Ally.”

Arms sales at risk

A sudden pullout would put allies in a bind, U.K. interviewees told RAND. In the short term, they would have little choice but to buy off-the-shelf equipment, often from U.S. suppliers, to fill gaps. Over the long term, broken trust would push allies toward European or homegrown alternatives, the authors concluded.

In Britain’s case, a sudden withdrawal would “accelerate the UK’s reorientation away from U.S. defense equipment toward sovereign or European alternatives,” those interviewees said. The authors expect Germany, and possibly Poland, to reach a similar conclusion.

Germany has bought U.S. arms partly to keep Washington committed to Europe. A rapid, uncoordinated withdrawal would suggest that approach had failed, and Berlin “may not see a benefit to continuing to privilege U.S. firms for arms sales or other deals,” the authors write.

“Such a shift would be detrimental to the U.S. defense industrial base, which benefits from exports that help create economies of scale,” the report said. “The main cause of this shift would be perceptions of U.S. reliability as an ally rather than the loss of any specific U.S. military capabilities.”

The U.S. supplied 58% of the major weapons European NATO members imported from 2021 to 2025, according to the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute, down from 64% in the institute’s previous five-year count.

U.S. soldiers parachute during Iron Wolf military exercises with NATO forces and partners on May 16, 2025, at the Gaiziunai training area near Rukla, Lithuania. (Paulius Peleckis/Getty Images)

In Monday’s statement, the five ministers also committed to “closer defence-industrial cooperation.” But British Defense Secretary Wes Streeting said Oct. 15 that buying European or American equipment “should not be an either/or choice.” British industry produces about 15% of each F-35, he told the Center for Strategic and International Studies in Washington.

A sudden U.S. pullout would likely have little impact on how much France buys from U.S. defense companies, as Paris is already “the most committed of the four allies to reducing reliance on the United States,” the study said.

During wartime, however, French officials told RAND their military would still count on U.S. forces for intelligence, surveillance and reconnaissance; long-range precision strike; fighter jets; air and missile defense; command and control, the systems used to direct forces in combat; and logistics.

Until France can fill those gaps itself, Paris would see a sudden U.S. pullout as a sign Washington cares little about its allies’ security, the authors wrote. That could make the two countries less likely to agree on military plans or diplomatic initiatives.

Intelligence sharing

The authors also looked at what would happen if the U.S. moved to normalize relations with Russia while the war in Ukraine continued, without addressing Europe’s security concerns. In that case, allies might limit intelligence sharing and joint military planning, fearing leaks to Moscow.

Some allies have already begun limiting intelligence sharing over other disagreements with Washington, albeit modestly, the report said. It cites U.K. concerns about U.S. military operations in the Caribbean, and Dutch concerns about U.S. policy toward Russia, which led both countries to reduce some intelligence sharing.

U.S. President Donald Trump speaks with Russian President Vladimir Putin as they meet to negotiate for an end to the war in Ukraine, at Joint Base Elmendorf-Richardson, Alaska, Aug. 15, 2025. (Kevin Lamarque/Reuters)

Still, none of the four allies would side with Russia or China on security under any scenario RAND examined, the authors concluded.

“Some strategists’ fears about allies aligning with Russia as the United States draws down are overblown,” the authors wrote. The four countries and Italy called Russia “the most acute and long-term threat to Euro-Atlantic security and stability” in Monday’s statement.

Spending gap

A limited pullback is more likely to spur allies to spend more and work more closely together, the authors found. All four have committed to NATO’s target of at least 3.5% of gross domestic product, or GDP, for core defense spending by 2035, according to the alliance’s 2025 summit declaration.

Poland has more than doubled defense spending as a share of GDP since 2014 and is aiming for 5% this year, the report said. Germany plans to spend €649 billion on its military over five years, raising spending from 2.4% of GDP in 2025 to 3.5% by 2029, Defense News reported.

Members of the Polish Air Force watch the arrival of one of three Lockheed Martin F-35A “Husarz” fighter jets at the 32nd Air Base in Lask, Poland, on May 22, 2026. (Damian Lemanski/Bloomberg via Getty Images)

France and the U.K. will struggle to meet NATO’s target because of high debt and slow growth, the report said. French public debt rose from 98% of GDP in 2019 to an average of 112% from 2020 to 2024. One interviewee called the budget problem France’s “Achilles’ heel” in rearming.

The U.K. Ministry of Defence asked for an extra £28 billion to cover a shortfall through 2030 and received £15 billion, the report said. The British government said Oct. 28 it would fully fund its Defence Investment Plan, Reuters reported.

Troops as leverage

The authors also warn against using troop levels to reward or punish individual allies. Questionnaires the Pentagon sent allies as part of the review asked about their defense spending and support for U.S. foreign policy priorities. Some allies read that as a sign troop decisions would hinge on political alignment, the report said.

Poland has already seen how quickly U.S. troop decisions can change. In May, the Pentagon abruptly canceled a planned deployment of about 4,000 soldiers to Poland. President Donald Trump later pledged to send 5,000 troops at an unspecified date. Warsaw credited the reversal partly to reminding Washington that Poland is a “model ally” that buys U.S. systems.

The authors said the episode demonstrates both the payoff Poland expects and the weakness of its approach: U.S. troop levels and commitments rest on “abrupt, easily reversible decisions.”

Call for a plan

“NATO 3.0 is a vision for a reduced U.S. role and reallocation of resources. But there is no concrete, long-term plan,” the report said.

The authors urge the administration to work with allies to set how many U.S. troops stay in Europe in peacetime and how many the U.S. would send in a war, along with a timeline to get there.

It is unclear whether the Europe review will produce such a plan. Under a Pentagon document seen by Reuters in August, the review is due to give Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth at least four sets of troop options by Nov. 6. The full review is due in December.

Predictable long-term plans that give allies a say “could sustain a continued, if reimagined, transatlantic partnership,” the authors wrote. Adding that treating troops and commitments as bargaining chips, by contrast, “could undercut regional cooperation and, in turn, NATO 3.0’s goal of European strength.”

Imagery captured by the European Space Agency’s freely available Sentinel-2 and Sentinel-1 satellites appears to be delayed over Gaza and parts of the West Bank, a Bellingcat analysis has found.

While the delay remains relatively short at just 24 hours, this can still impact the speed at which analysts and researchers can observe what is happening in the region.

For example, the Israeli Defence Forces demolished homes in the West Bank Palestinian city of Hebron in recent days. New imagery captured on October 26, which included Hebron, would allow open source researchers to quickly confirm the demolitions, assess the impacted area and observe any unreported IDF activity. But imagery for the western half of Hebron was not available until October 27 due to the Sentinel-2 delay, inhibiting real-time fact-checking and reporting on this evolving situation.

A spokesperson for the European Union told Bellingcat that the delay was not related to events in Gaza but to “mitigate possible threats” due to the war in the Middle East “including Iran’s attacks on its neighbours and possible threats to European interests”. The delayed region of Sentinel tiles that cover Gaza as well as parts of Israel and the West Bank also extends over the Eastern Mediterranean Sea.

The European Union ordered a 24-hour delay on Sentinel satellite imagery over the Gulf of Oman earlier this year during US and Israeli strikes on Iran. However the delay impacting Gaza and parts of the West Bank has not been announced or previously reported.

“Such decisions are taken unanimously by all EU Member states and will stay in place as long as necessary,” the spokesperson said. They also said that while the Copernicus program, which includes the Sentinel satellites, operated a free and open data policy there were limitations to this for security. 

When asked if the delay, which began in late March, was related to the presence of US vessels in the Eastern Mediterranean or if there were other areas of delay, the spokesperson said they had no further comment.

Sentinel Delays

The Sentinel-2 and Sentinel-1 satellites provide an invaluable source of imagery for open source researchers. This has been especially true this year, as commercial satellite imagery providers restricted access to imagery in the Middle East, including areas of active human rights concern, such as Gaza. 

However, in July, the European Union published Council Decision 2026/1716, which ordered a 24-hour delay on Sentinel imagery over the Gulf of Oman, after a request from the US government which was involved in strikes on Iran at that time.

Just two weeks later, the European Union issued a corrigendum that unpublished decision 2026/1716 and removed it from the Council of the European Union record, although satellite data shows the delay has remained. 

This decision, while it was available, was reportedly the first public notification of delayed Sentinel imagery. Yet it was not the first time that Sentinel imagery has been restricted, analysis shows. Bellingcat found four distinct regions of delayed satellite imagery during 2025 and 2026: the Gulf of Oman, the Gulf of Aden/western Arabian Sea, the Red Sea as well as the previously unreported delay in the Eastern Mediterranean Sea and parts of Israel, the West Bank and the entirety of Gaza.

Bellingcat’s analysis shows the regions where Sentinel-2 imagery is subject to a 24-hour delay, and when the delay was imposed. Only the July 15, 2026 delay over the Gulf of Oman was referenced by European Commission directives and the region referenced in 2026/1716 encompasses only a portion of the delayed tiles.

Bellingcat’s analysis shows that these delays have been implemented in stages. Twenty-four hour delays of Sentinel-2 imagery were first observed by open source researchers in March and April 2025 after the US launched a naval and air strike campaign against Houthi targets in Yemen. 

The previously unreported imagery delay in the Eastern Mediterranean began on March 28, 2026, the same month that commercial American space imaging company Planet Labs also restricted imagery across the Middle East. The Gulf of Oman delay began on July 15, 2026, two days after the publication of Council Decision 2026/1716.

Decision 2026/1716 also specified that the delay be implemented for both Sentinel-2 and Sentinel-1 radar imagery. Bellingcat’s analysis of Sentinel-1 image availability shows that this is the case for both the Gulf of Oman region and the unreported region over the Eastern Mediterranean Sea. However, the 2025 restrictions on Sentinel-2 imagery in the Red Sea and Gulf of Aden are not implemented in Sentinel-1. 

Sentinel-1 imagery uses “synthetic aperture radar” to capture lower resolution radar images that can collect data at night and on cloudy days. This differs from Sentinel-2, which captures optical satellite imagery at higher resolution.

A Tale of Two Tiles

Sentinel satellite imagery is most commonly accessed by open source researchers through Copernicus Browser, a free online platform, but it is also distributed as large tiled files in the Copernicus Data Space Ecosystem. These tiles are numbered using the Military Grid Reference System, and can be listed using APIs that provide information on each tile, including the capture and publication time. By comparing the capture and publication time, the publication delay can be computed.

For example, the catalog information for the tile T36RXV, which includes Gaza and portions of the West Bank, Negev and Egypt, provides the following metadata for the October 21, 2026 capture.

Metadata from tile T36RXV, which includes Gaza and portions of the West Bank, Negev and Egypt.

This shows that while the image was captured at 08:20 UTC on October 21, it was not published until 10:09 UTC on October 22 — almost 26 hours later. This is in contrast to the neighboring tile T36RYV, which includes parts of the West Bank, Israel, and Jordan. API data for this tile shows that the image was published at 12:10 UTC on October 21, just under four hours after the capture time.

Metadata from tile T36RYV, which includes parts of the West Bank, Israel, and Jordan.

This difference is not unique to this date either. Bellingcat’s analysis shows that this tile has been consistently delayed by one day since March 28, 2026.

Graphing the publication delay for two adjacent Sentinel-2 tiles shows a clear difference beginning March 28, 2026.

This method can be extended further, and by analysing every tile with valid publication date timestamps, Bellingcat identified all global Sentinel-2 and Sentinel-1 delays since 2023. The only consistent delays visible were those identified in the Gulf of Oman, the Gulf of Aden/western Arabian Sea, the Red Sea and Eastern Mediterranean in 2025 and 2026.

Impact on open source investigations

While the 24-hour delay on certain Sentinel imagery is a less severe restriction than other satellite providers have imposed, it can still impact the open source research process in fast moving situations. This is especially so in recent months as the European Space Agency’s Sentinel satellite data has emerged as a refuge as other satellite data is censored.
