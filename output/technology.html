‘Open the hives’ — Why France is turning tank gunners into FPV pilots

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

MAILLY-LE-CAMP, France — Corporal Igor started a new job this year, swapping the sights of a 105 mm gun on the French Army’s AMX 10 RC reconnaissance vehicle for first-person view goggles and a handheld controller.

The 21-year-old is now a strike pilot in France’s first dedicated drone-combat squadron, crouching in nettles and tall grass as he guided an FPV drone down a forest path at the Mailly-le-Camp training area east of Paris during a June exercise of the 1st Marine Infantry Regiment, or 1er RIMa.

“We’re switching specialties, we’re keeping up with the times,” said the corporal, who last year still manned the gunner seat in the AMX 10 RC armored car.

Inspired by the war in Ukraine, the 1er RIMa converted one of its three light cavalry squadrons into an experimental all-drone unit, with results so convincing that the Army is rushing more drone formations into service. The aim is for around 15 drone-combat units by summer 2027 and more by the end of next year, Army Chief of Staff Gen. Jacques de Montgros said this month.

France deployed a drone-combat unit to Romania for the first time in recent weeks as part of Mission Aigle, the French-led mission to strengthen NATO’s eastern flank, and will deploy a second unit to Poland for an exercise next month, de Montgros told lawmakers in an Oct. 14 hearing. “So we’re really at a point of scaling up our efforts in these areas.”

Cpl. Igor of the French Army’s 1st Marine Infantry Regiment operates an FPV drone during a training exercise at Mailly-le-Camp on June 11, 2026. (Rudy Ruitenberg/staff)

The 1er RIMa’s experimental squadron has provided a blueprint to create more tactical drone formations, while offering the French Army a lesson in the vulnerability of NATO-style combined-arms tactics to drone threats. During exercises this year, the regiment’s pilots hunted armored vehicles and command posts of the opposing force, disrupting maneuver and force buildup.

Mixing surveillance drones, FPV one-way attack drones and loitering munitions, the 1er Escadron de Drones de Chasse, which roughly translates as 1st Hunter-Drone Squadron, has also been a test bed for the Army, partnering with French firms to work on swarming technology, autonomous terminal guidance and launch canisters.

France’s first drone squadron grew from an idea within the 1er RIMa’s training and operations office in late 2023, to develop a way of fighting with drones that went beyond just adding them to existing units. The concept built on a proposal by Capt. Guillaume, an open-source intelligence specialist who had been studying the war in Ukraine.

When the office tested an experimental drone unit in a tactical exercise, the drones created “a crazy amount of disruption,” said Col. Aymeric Caussin, commander of the 1er RIMa, who headed the office at the time. “So we said, right, let’s go for it.”

Cavalry heritage

Drawing on the regiment’s light cavalry heritage, the squadron was designed as a mobile force with drones as its weapons system, said Lt. Col Remi, who worked to implement the idea during a two-year stint in the 1er RIMa. Light cavalry principles still applied: gather intelligence, then fix, outflank and ultimately destroy the enemy, with a focus on mobility, dispersion and stealth.

“We needed a squadron that evoked the light cavalry: ‘drone’ because that’s its primary weapon system, and ‘hunter’ because that’s what we actually do in the field.”

The regiment tested the concept during Exercise Hedgehog in Estonia in May 2025, sending some 10 drone pilots as part of a French combined-arms tactical group. The U.S. 5th squadron, 7th Cavalry Regiment served as the designated opposing force during the exercise, with dismounted soldiers and M2A4 Bradley Fighting Vehicles.

The Wall Street Journal previously reported how Ukrainian operators taking part in the exercise exposed the vulnerability of NATO troops to drone warfare. The French team equally showed the impact of drones on an opponent unprepared for the threat.

The French pilots, who made up around 5% of the tactical group’s active strength, accounted for 20% of the attrition inflicted on the opposing force of American troops with Bradleys in a single day of combat, said Maj. Sylvain, head of operations at the 1er RIMa, who attended the exercise.

The virtual devastation convinced French Army leaders to scale up the experiment. Within two months, the drone squadron was made official, and in early April 2026 the Army staff declared it “fully operational.”

“It was the proof of concept,” Maj. Sylvain said. “Everything falls into place, everyone is supportive of the project, and the regiment decides to transform the first squadron into a hunter-drone squadron.”

The drone team also brought several startups to Estonia for Hedgehog 25, including Alta Ares and Icarus Swarms, while Naval Group joined at its own expense. The 1er RIMa works with Alta Ares on AI-enabled detection and reconnaissance, Icarus Swarms on autonomous navigation and computer-assisted piloting, and Naval Group on drone-launch canisters.

The drone squadron has continue to rack up the virtual kills since Hedgehog. During France’s large-scale exercise Orion 2026, the 1er RIMa’s drone pilots tallied four regimental command posts, a number of logistics convoys and anti-drone and air-defense units, according to Caussin.

‘Quite a bit of damage’

What made the drone-combat unit stand out was its responsiveness, with FPV drones able to quickly close gaps in the lines and attack the main axis of enemy efforts, and its ability to disrupt the enemy command structures, particularly by directly targeting command posts, Caussin said.

“Through the drone, we’ll seek to disrupt the enemy’s chain of command. We’re influencing decision-making.”

Orion 2026 showed that distance no longer protects. The squadron’s intelligence, surveillance and reconnaissance drones detected tactical command posts several kilometers from the front line, then called in artillery or directed FPV drones to attack, said Staff Sgt. Tristan, who leads a platoon of FPV pilots after about 12 years on the AMX 10 RC.

Troops of the French Army’s 1st Marine Infantry Regiment launch an FPV drone from a canister during an exercise at the Mailly-le-Camp training grounds on June 11, 2026. (Rudy Ruitenberg/staff)

The regiment inflicted “quite a bit of damage” with drones and artillery, “but had it been the other way around, it would have been the same,” Staff Sgt. Tristan said. “If we’d been facing a unit like ours and we would have stayed in the AMX 10 RC, well, we would’ve been wiped out a lot too, right? So you have to put things in perspective.”

During an exercise in March at Mailly-le-Camp, the 1er RIMa acted as the opposing force during the combat preparation of another French light-cavalry regiment. The drone squadron’s pilots attacked and virtually destroyed enemy armor, or would track vehicles to command posts or troop concentrations before striking, paralyzing enemy movement.

The result was “severe,” said FPV pilot Igor, who participated in the exercise. The opposing force was neutralized, at least virtually.

For the Army, the takeaway was that it wanted to equip five more regiments with an all-drone squadron, to be operational in 2027. With the Army accelerating its drone effort, the six combat-drone units will be operational by the year, said Gen. Bruno Baratz, head of Future Combat Command, at an innovation day organized by France’s 6th Armored Brigade in September.

The brigade, which was assigned to set up two of the five drone squadrons, decided to create a third drone-combat unit using its own resources, 6th Armored Brigade commander Gen. Maxime do Tran said at the innovation day. One of the brigade’s infantry regiments is now deployed to Romania with a drone-combat unit.

“The trial conducted by the 1st Marine Infantry Regiment demonstrated the value of this capability,” Do Tran said. “We didn’t wait.”

With the drone-combat squadron of the 1er RIMa “more of a cavalry, reconnaissance” unit, Do Tran says the 6th Armored Brigade has taken a slightly different approach for its infantry regiments, ensuring that drone pilots previously trained on the Akeron antitank missile or 81-millimeter mortars retain those skills through dual training.

He said the brigade staff realized that whenever the weather was unfavorable for drone operations, those dual capabilities were needed to retain lethality. Creating drone units also created a problem of mass, according to Do Tran.

During the brigade’s first combat-preparation exercises with combat-drones, commanders found that even if their drone pilots achieved initial saturation, a numerically strong enemy would still break through lines that lacked ground troops to stop a large force.

“We quickly get overwhelmed if we don’t also have men behind them with rifles or other weapons,” Do Tran said. “We need to maintain a certain balance between human mass and drone pilots.”

Combat-drone units will have different uses based on the regiment they’re part of, according to the general.

He said that for a light cavalry regiment such as the 1st RIMa, drones contribute to reconnaissance and intelligence gathering, while for an infantry regiment, the primary role will be delivering lethal firepower. For a regiment of Leclerc main battle tanks, the combat drone unit’s primary role is to protect the armored column, Do Tran said.

“It’s an entirely new set of rules to learn, an entirely new combination of capabilities to reinvent,” said Baratz. “The Army’s task today is to reinvent itself to be effective in this modern combat.”

Drone vulnerabilities

“We shouldn’t think that drones will make the old systems obsolete. Drones do have vulnerabilities: weather conditions, electronic jamming, other counter-drone systems being put in place. We still need our older systems for deep-strike operations, close-contact maneuvers and direct engagement.”

Officers of the 1er RIMa cautioned against over-interpreting the March exercise, with Maj. Sylvain saying the opposing regiment lacked adequate anti-drone capabilities and had few drones of its own, making an effective response difficult. Caussin likewise noted that during Orion, the exercise in free terrain limited realistic use of jamming by the opposing force.

Equipment used by the French Army’s 1st Marine Infantry Regiment is on display during an exercise at the Mailly-le-Camp training grounds on June 11, 2026. (Rudy Ruitenberg/staff)

“If we’re back in a tank tomorrow and opposite of us we have another drone unit, it’s going to be tough for us as well,” Staff Sgt. Tristan said. “So we need all this feedback to figure out how to fight back against that.”

For its own combat preparation in June, the 1er RIMa asked the Centac combined-arms training center that runs the exercises at Mailly-le-Camp to step up the challenge, particularly by creating a contested electronic warfare environment.

The training center also added 12-gauge shotguns to the infantry kit and emphasized camouflage. Soldiers unfolded camouflaged umbrellas bought at sporting goods store Decathlon whenever they were on the move, seeking to obscure their visual signature.

The umbrellas slowed the 1er RIMa’s drone operators, forcing them to spend more time identifying targets to avoid confusion with the numerous deer or boars at Mailly-le-Camp, said Lt. Col. Guillaume, head of training at the 1st Light Infantry Battalion that staffs the center.

The opposing force also remained more dispersed, avoided grouped movement and kept vehicles farther back, bringing them forward only when the situation was considered sufficiently safe. The transparency provided by drones clearly changed how the exercise unfolded and affected the conduct of operations, Lt. Col. Guillaume said.

While the drone advantage was less pronounced in June than in March, the 1er RIMa’s drone squadron obtained the best score of the roughly 30 Army units that passed through the Mailly-le-Camp training center over a 12-month period, Lt. Col. Guillaume said. The June exercise provided a better assessment of how the drone squadron fares against a more capable adversary, according to Caussin.

The regiment explored what might happen if the tactical deadlock observed in Ukraine were broken and maneuver is restored, with Caussin saying the drone squadron’s mobility would allow it to move rapidly between different parts of the battlefield, using drones to understand the tactical situation and fill gaps.

But the exercise also showed the battlefield overview provided by drones, while necessary to win an engagement, is not enough by itself. The speed at which intelligence can be converted into action is as important as the number of drones in the air, and traditional infantry elements remained necessary to provide direct kinetic action, Lt. Col. Guillaume said.

Jam detection

Electronic warfare reduced drone effectiveness on several occasions, allowing the opposing force to bring in armor to create a temporary advantage and secure objectives, said Lt. Col. Guillaume, describing a constant “game of cat and mouse.”

Because of the energy requirements of electronic warfare, jamming is not necessarily continuous. The regiment learned it needed to become better at determining when it was actually being jammed by the adversary, and when a failed maneuver was due to its own poor execution, according to Caussin.

For the regimental commander, the exercise confirmed the drone squadron complements rather than replaces armored formations, an additional layer to combined-arms warfare than a revolution. With the opponent better prepared for the drone threat, the June exercise forced the 1er RIMa to change how it employed drones, picking priority targets rather than observing and striking at will.

In a drone-saturated environment, advantage will come more from controlling the pace of events rather than simply holding ground, by combining drones and highly mobile forces to observe, adapt, and act faster than the adversary, according to Caussin.

He called the stand-out quality of the drone-combat unit its ability “manipulate the enemy’s decision-making time, really to overwhelm them” and allow the regiment to seize the initiative.

“Will the drone’s enemy be another drone?” Caussin said. “We’re starting to implement this to some extend in small-scale exercises, particularly at the regimental level.”

“It’s always difficult to talk about game-changers. What’s certain today is that this is the reality of the battlefield and it enables us to achieve tactical effects that have a decisive impact on the enemy.”

In the 1er RIMa, FVP pilots are teamed with a driver, a vehicle commander and optionally a fourth crew member operating a drone by Paris-based vendor Parrot, or other ISR system to designate targets. Cpl. Igor said he often works with one crew member as a spotter. The teams still carry Heckler & Koch rifles, a heavy machine gun to defend against enemy infantry, and an AT4 shoulder-fired antitank weapon.

Farther reach

With the 105 mm gun of the AMX 10 RC, “we had reach, but we were much more visible,” Staff Sgt. Tristan said. Now, “we hole up in a corner with our vehicle and launch our drones from hiding, so the enemy can’t see us. With an AMX 10 RC, we can’t do that.”

The regiment’s drone squadron retained the structure of an armored reconnaissance unit, with a command and logistics platoon and four combat platoons: an ISR unit, two FPV drone units and one platoon operating longer-range loitering munitions.

With around 100 drone pilots in a unit of 120 personnel, the squadron can provide intelligence and strike across an area typically covered by a formation several times larger, with an operational reach approaching that of a full combined-arms tactical group, according to Lt. Col. Remi.

A team from the French Army’s 1st Marine Infantry Regiment operates an FPV drone during an exercise at the Mailly-le-Camp training grounds on June 11, 2026. (Rudy Ruitenberg/staff)

The large number of drone operators changes logistics. Supply vehicles carry drones rather than ammunition, and the unit needs battlefield energy generation to keep batteries topped up. FPV pilot Igor said that during a day of exercise and simulated strikes, he might go through 10 or 20 drones.

Both Caussin and Maj. Sylvain emphasized drones alone will not decide a battle. The squadron is designed to operate alongside conventional units and slot into larger formations, with interoperability with French Army units and NATO allies built in from the start, said Maj. Sylvain.

In addition to developing doctrine, the regiment’s mandate is to work with industrial partners on technologies to make the drone squadron more effective. Once concept, described by officers and pilots as “queen and workers,” involves an operator controlling a mother drone, or queen, which in turn steers multiple worker drones launched from a “hive,” or canister.

The goal is essentially fire-and-forget autonomous engagement, according to Maj. Sylvain. An operator assigns a mission, the queen autonomously seeks and identifies the designated target, then directs worker drones to destroy it without further operator intervention.

With the squadron conceived as a mobile force rather than one dug in like Ukrainian operators, the idea is to have four hives at the back of a vehicle, with a stock of drones ready to deploy, Staff Sgt. Tristan said.

“Just open the hives, send out four drones on autopilot, and let them carry out their mission.”

While “not everything works perfect,” the squadron and its industry partners have figured out queen-worker communications and terminal guidance, Maj. Sylvain said.

FPV drone hit rates might be 5% to 10% for a novice pilot and 30% for an experienced one, compared with a more than 90% chance to land a shot with the AMX 10 RC cannon, according to Staff Sgt. Tristan. The regiment is counting on autonomous terminal guidance to increase drone hit rates to around 80% to 90%.

The squadron’s doctrinal goals include FPV strikes at a distance of 10 kilometers, and communication range is the main practical obstacle, Maj. Sylvain said. The ISR platoon uses several drone models from Parrot and is starting to achieve the ranges of up to 20 kilometers called for in the doctrine.

Officers in the 1er RIMa shied away from comparisons with Ukrainian drone units, which Maj. Sylvain said are battle-hardened after several years of war.

‘We’re not naive’

“It’s obvious that we’d learn a lot from an exercise against them,” he said. “We’re not naive. We’re fully aware of our current level of capability, and we know we still have a lot of work to do to be ready to deploy the drone-combat squadron in a high-intensity conflict.”

The June exercise was also an opportunity to work on defense against enemy UAVs.

“Camouflage ourselves, take cover, and make sure there’s always someone listening out for drones, either to destroy them with certain weapons or simply to hide,” Staff Sgt. Tristan said. “In the heat of battle, chasing down a vehicle, also staying alert for anti-drone is difficult.”

The constant drone threat changes daily life for soldiers on exercise. Rather than having fixed meal times, “you have to snack throughout the day,” said Staff Sgt. Tristan. Sleep also becomes fragmented, with drone surveillance continuing through the night.

The 1er RIMa itself has adapted its command structure to the drone presence, dividing up command posts and deploying in the same armored vehicles as combat units to avoid detection.

“We question ourselves, we wonder how we can protect ourselves from this too,” said Cpl. Igor, the FPV pilot. “If we’re capable of doing it, we’re not the only ones who know how. So we’re going to try to find ways to protect ourselves from drones, because they’re really effective.”

A GJ-21 stealthy flying-wing uncrewed combat air vehicle (UCAV) has successfully launched from and recovered aboard the Type 076 amphibious assault ship Sichuan, according to China’s People’s Liberation Army (PLA). Visual confirmation of this milestone has yet to emerge, but there have been steady signs of preparations for flight trials from the Sichuan, which features an electromagnetic catapult. GJ-21s have already been spotted flying from airfields on land.

A picture of a GJ-21 drone that previously emerged online. Chinese internet

The GJ-21 and the land-based GJ-11 Mysterious Dragon it is based on are prime examples of China’s major investments in UCAVs and other flying-wing-type drones. These developments stand in particular contrast to the U.S. military’s eschewing of such designs, at least publicly. This is despite significant past U.S. achievements in this arena, including in the development of carrier-based UCAVs. The PLA Navy (PLAN) now looks to be on track to be the first country to field an operational capability of this kind.

A stock picture of the Type 076 amphibious assault ship Sichuan. PLA

“China’s first ship-borne stealth reconnaissance and attack drone ‘GJ-21’ recently made its first successful take-off and landing on [the Type] 076 amphibious assault ship, the ‘Sichuan’ (Hull 51),” according to a post today from the China Military Bugle account on X, an official PLA mouthpiece. “This is another significant breakthrough in the development of China’s amphibious assault ship, indicating that the Chinese Navy now has the capability to operate fixed-wing stealth drones from large maritime combat platforms.”

At the time of writing, neither the PLA nor Chinese state media have shared where or when these trials occurred or provided any other additional details, including how many total launches and recoveries were conducted. No official pictures or videos from the testing have been released so far.

As mentioned earlier, the continued development of the GJ-11 and GJ-21 are reflective of the PLA’s broader pursuit of stealthy flying-wing drones, which is not limited to UCAVs. Several larger designs that look most immediately suited to high-altitude, long-endurance ISR missions have also broken cover in the country in the past few years.

There is something of a trend in this regard globally, with Russia, India, Turkey, and France also pursuing flying-wing drone designs. The U.S. military continues to be markedly absent from this space, at least that we know, despite major investments and real demonstrations of relevant capability, before various programs were abandoned for reasons that still remain largely unclear. In the late 2010s, the U.S. Navy notably looked to be right on the edge of fielding a carrier-based UCAV, before changing course entirely to an uncrewed tanker instead. There are some signs now that the service may be taking another look at the capabilities it passed over.

Highlights From North Korea’s Drone-A-Palooza

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

North Korean leader Kim Jong Un presided over a major exhibition of uncrewed aerial vehicles (UAVs) on Thursday. A mockup of an apparent new large stealth drone was displayed prominently alongside an increasingly diverse collection of reconnaissance drones, armed UAVs, loitering munitions, and small attack drones. While there is no evidence that North Korea is capable of getting a stealthy flying-wing drone flying, let alone operating as a operationally relevant low-observable platform, there are no doubts that the country has an increasingly impressive uncrewed aircraft arsenal, with the smaller, lower-end types being of greatest importance.

An overview of the Unmanned Aeronautical Technology Development-2026 exhibition, with the “stealth drone” in pride of place. KCNA

As for the “stealth drone,” photographs released by North Korean state media show a mockup of a tailless flying-wing design. In terms of appearance, there are clear parallels with advanced uncrewed combat aerial vehicles (UCAVs) developed by the United States, China, and Russia.

However, huge questions remain as to what degree the mockup is part of a realistic program. We have no details of the program’s development status and planned capabilities. It is highly questionable if Pyongyang is able to produce a functional prototype. Even if it can get it into the air, there is no evidence that it possesses the capabilities to master meaningful stealth technologies, let alone the command-and-control functions and other elements needed for it to operate in a tactically relevant manner.

The exhibition, titled Unmanned Aeronautical Technology Development-2026, was held on Thursday, with images published by the ruling Workers’ Party newspaper Rodong Sinmun today. Kim called for the country to achieve “absolute superiority” in drone technology and announced unspecified measures to accelerate the development of uncrewed aviation systems and military artificial intelligence.

Kim Jong Un walks past rows of quadcopter drones at the exhibition. Rodong Sinmun

An Ambitious New Flying-Wing Design

The mockup of the new aircraft was positioned prominently in the exhibition hall, suggesting that Pyongyang considers it at least indicative of its broader ambitions.

Based on the available photographs, Hong Min, a senior research fellow at the Korea Institute for National Unification, interviewed by NK News, estimated the wingspan at roughly 56 to 66 feet and its height at around 10 to 11.5 feet. Standout features include a dorsal air intake, a relatively large center fuselage section integrated into the flying wing, and retractable tricycle landing gear.

Another view of North Korea’s apparent new stealth drone. KCTV

In terms of general arrangement, there are similarities to Northrop Grumman’s X-47B, the U.S. Navy’s experimental carrier-based UCAV. There are also visual similarities with China’s GJ-11 Sharp Sword and Russia’s S-70 Okhotnik, two other large flying-wing UCAV designs.

X-47B during carrier operations. U.S. Navy GJ-11 Sharp Sword UCAV. via Chinese internet

At this point, North Korea has not provided any technical information about the aircraft’s propulsion system, payload, intended mission, or flight performance.

Critically, while the external shape is consistent with a stealthy UCAV, a flying-wing configuration on its own is only one part of a far more complex puzzle in achieving a genuinely low radar signature. Other factors include the engine inlet and exhaust arrangements, radar-absorbing materials, surface treatments, manufacturing tolerances, and the alignment of individual edges and surfaces, low probability of intercept communications and much more.

Officials inspect the new flying-wing UCAV design. Rodong Sinmun

The Iranian RQ-170 Precedent

Overall, there are many good reasons to be extremely cautious about North Korea’s apparent ambitions in this area.

Iran is another country that has displayed a drone that resembles an advanced Western stealth platform, without actually reproducing its capabilities. It has, however, gotten a UCAV-lookalike into the air.

After recovering a U.S. RQ-170 Sentinel reconnaissance drone that came down inside Iranian territory in 2011, Tehran announced that it had successfully reverse-engineered the aircraft. In 2014, Iranian authorities displayed what they presented as a domestically produced copy of the American drone, claiming that it would soon undertake a test flight.

The actual RQ-170 Sentinel Iran recovered on display. Iranian State Media

Outside observers questioned whether the displayed aircraft was anything more than a non-functional replica, noting apparent shortcomings in its construction and the use of components inconsistent with a sophisticated operational aircraft.

Iran subsequently presented a family of aircraft derived from the RQ-170 concept, including the Shahed-171 Simorgh and the smaller Shahed-191. Iran has demonstrated flying examples of these designs, but they fall far short of replicating the RQ-170’s actual low-observable qualities, let alone its sensors and avionics.

Iran’s self-proclaimed RQ-170-like long-range UCAVs. AP

In the case of Pyongyang, however, the possibility of Russian assistance is at least worth considering.

North Korea’s burgeoning military relationship with Moscow, including the deployment of North Korean troops to support Russia’s war in Ukraine, could provide a chance for the exchange of technical knowledge and operational experience from Moscow. Russia has developed and flown the S-70 Okhotnik UCAV, exposing it to some operational trials in Ukraine, and could have passed related information on to Pyongyang.

An S-70 taxiing after landing. Russian Ministry of Defense

In particular, North Korea would welcome Russian assistance in developing and manufacturing propulsion, flight control, sensor, and materials technologies.

However, there is so far no proof of technology transfer from Russia. Even if it were to be provided, it is far from clear that this would suffice to get a true UCAV into the air anytime soon, although an Iran-style knockoff with far reduced capabilities cannot be ruled out entirely. It’s also worth noting that Russia’s own UCAV program has been anything but a smooth and rapid advancement to an operational state.

Overall, the new flying-wing concept does not represent a realistic technological undertaking — at least for now. Very few countries have managed to develop and fly drones in this class, and fewer still have been able to field them operationally.

North Korea’s Drone Development Effort

The flying-wing aircraft was only one part of a much larger display. More than 30 types of UAVs reportedly appeared at the exhibition, reflecting North Korea’s efforts to diversify its uncrewed aviation capabilities.

Some of these designs had been seen before, including the Saetbyol-4 and Saetbyol-9. These were first displayed in 2023 and are, in terms of appearance, clones of the U.S. RQ-4 Global Hawk and MQ-9 Reaper, respectively.

Kim Jong Un inspects an armed Saetbyol-9. Rodong Sinmun

Imagery released of the Saetbyol-9 showed it carrying — and firing — several types of air-to-ground weapons. These include a Hellfire-like laser-guided missile, along with smaller rockets and other possible guided munitions.

Saetbyol-9 fires a rocket or missile. Rodong Sinmun

Other exhibits included loitering munitions resembling Israel’s Harop and Russia’s Lancet, as well as smaller drones with configurations similar to the U.S.-made Switchblade family.

Kim Jong Un inspecting Switchblade-style loitering munitions. Rodong Sinmun

Hundreds of small quadcopter drones were also displayed on storage racks. Some appeared to carry cylindrical containers associated with fiber-optic control systems, a technology that has become increasingly prominent in the Russia-Ukraine war. Unlike conventional radio-controlled drones, fiber-optic systems are largely immune to conventional radio-frequency jamming of their control links. These drones would be a major feature in a cross-DMZ conflict.

North Korean interest in artificial intelligence was another recurring theme. Kim called for the development of increasingly intelligent uncrewed weapons, although there is no indication of how advanced the country’s existing autonomous navigation, target recognition, or engagement systems actually are.

The exhibition was accompanied by live-fire demonstrations involving UAVs, cruise missiles, and multiple rocket launchers. North Korean state media claimed that missiles launched from drones and other systems struck an island target “with 100 percent accuracy.”

A barrage of rockets fired by a large-caliber multiple rocket launcher. Rodong Sinmun

From Ambition To Operational Capabilities

North Korea’s rapidly expanding interest in drones should also be seen against the shortcomings of its aging crewed combat aviation fleet. Relatively inexpensive UAVs offer opportunities for reconnaissance and attacks against military infrastructure without exposing pilots to enemy air defenses. At the same time, Pyongyang has failed to introduce new crewed combat aircraft to modernize its air force, the capabilities of which are thoroughly outmatched by those of South Korea.

The war in Ukraine (as well as lessons from conflicts in the Middle East) also provides Pyongyang with an opportunity to study the use of drones on a large scale. North Korean troops deployed to Russia will have gained first-hand experience of drone warfare, including the integration of reconnaissance UAVs, FPV attack drones, and electronic warfare systems.

That experience could help accelerate the development of North Korea’s own uncrewed platforms, particularly relatively simple systems that can be produced in large quantities.

An attack drone in the Shahed/Geran class, for example, could be produced relatively easily in very large numbers (and that is likely already happening to some degree) and could saturate South Korean air defenses, which are generally tailored to address more conventional threats. North Korea doesn’t need the long range that the Shahed/Geran typically provides, since many of its primary targets are much closer. The shorter distances involved could permit a greater proportion of a drone’s weight to be allocated to its warhead, depending on the design. Alternatively, a drone with the roughly 1,000-mile reach of the Shahed/Geran could fly a very complex route to reach targets in the South, including approaching via the coast. At the very least, expanding North Korean efforts in this area could tilt the balance of power on the peninsula, providing Seoul with a major interdiction problem and forcing the development of new countermeasures, such as directed-energy weapons and cheap interceptors.

A typical Russian Shahed-136/Geran-2-series kamikaze drone over Ukraine. via X

The possibility of Russian assistance via its own Geran development effort is likely bringing enhanced technologies within reach of North Korea. Indeed, that kind of expertise is already being gained by tens of thousands of North Korean workers who have been sent to Russia as additional labor for its drone-production efforts.

A look inside a Russian factory where licensed production of Iranian Shahed-series one-way attack drones is taking place. via X

And once again, it’s these one-way attack munitions and smaller drones that can be mass-produced and rapidly iterated upon that pose a real threat to South Korea. It is likely that North Korea will continue to ramp-up its investment in these systems, building them in very large tranches in order to create an asymmetric advantage — one that has proven challenging to counter in real conflicts around the globe. At the same time, they will also continue to build high-tech-looking clones of far more advanced systems which will have little tactical or strategic impact but work well for propaganda purposes.

General Dynamics revealed a new artificial intelligence-enabled, portable communications system on Thursday called the Vion, a Mission Partner Environment solution. Although its basic purpose is to give ground troops a suitcase-sized platform to exchange messages and files, the company said its real value is how it connects allied systems. 

In an interview with Military Times, Jason Myers, vice president of defense agencies at General Dynamics Information Technology, explained that Vion can help ground troops quickly establish secure connections with a command center and coalition forces from remote environments.

Using open architecture and AI, he said Vion will connect with outside networks, adapt to or patch connections and translate written messages, voice memos, videos and more, all while maintaining zero-trust cybersecurity protections. 

“We wanted to provide an all-encompassing offering, but we didn’t want to lock somebody into an offering or not be able to use where they’ve already invested,” Myers said. 

He added that most organizations want a custom communications kit, and most companies aim to provide platforms that only work with proprietary equipment and software. But those things can make it challenging and expensive to update or upgrade systems. 

“What we wanted to do is say, ‘Yes, we’re going to use proven technologies,’ but as new things come out, we are absolutely ripping and replacing those, and how to do that securely, in that modular architecture, is really, really different than how things are being done today,” he said. 

According to the company, GDIT participated in a Joint Staff J6-led demonstration called Olympus Fires, where the Defense Information Systems Agency, or DISA, explored ways to improve the Coalition Information Environment, or CIE, which it described as “a modernized architecture built on the principles of Zero Trust and data centricity.”

At a panel discussion in June, U.S. Army Lt. Col. David Courter, DISA’s Combatant Command Integration and Plans chief, explained the goal of the event was to “broker and federate our environments together without replicating a new environment every time.”

“We’ve spent the past 50 years building siloed networks, siloed applications and siloed environments to collaborate internally with our own data. We have to talk to one another … We have to operate and maintain command and control with a single voice.”

Myers said GDIT is currently working through the acquisitions process for Vion but declined to say with whom. However, he said he thinks Vion, in terms of service, will “have a very long life in where it can add value to the mission.”

The United States and Japan will establish a facility in Japan for the final assembly and inspection of AIM-120 Advanced Medium-Range Air-to-Air Missiles (AMRAAMs), advancing a bilateral effort to expand munitions production as demand rises across the U.S. and allied military forces.

Japanese Defense Minister Shinjiro Koizumi announced the agreement following discussions with U.S. Secretary of War Pete Hegseth on the sidelines of the ASEAN Defense Ministers’ Meeting-Plus in Cebu, Philippines. The facility will operate under the broader Operation Supercharge initiative, which seeks to accelerate cooperation between the two countries on defense equipment and technology.

Secretary of War Pete Hegseth shakes hands with Japanese Minister of Defense Shinjirō Koizumi at the 13th ASEAN Defense Ministers’ Meeting-Plus in Cebu, Philippines, on October 28, 2026. DOW photo by U.S. Air Force Master Sgt. Brycen Guerrero

“We finally reached an agreement on the establishment of a final assembly and inspection facility in Japan for the AMRAAM air-to-air missile, which is essential for the F-35,” Koizumi said, according to a translation of his remarks published by Air & Space Forces Magazine.

Koizumi described the agreement as a means of ensuring access to equipment required by both countries while strengthening Japan’s domestic defense industrial base. At this point, the facility’s location, construction timeline, anticipated production capacity, and Japanese industrial partner are yet to be disclosed.

U.S. Air Force weapons load crew secure an AMRAAM onto a bomb lift truck at Nellis Air Force Base, Nevada, June 23, 2025. U.S. Air Force photo by William R. Lewis

The announcement follows a September award to RTX subsidiary Raytheon for a five-year AMRAAM production contract, with options for two additional years, valued at up to $20.7 billion. Under a framework agreement announced in February, Raytheon is working toward annual production of at least 1,900 missiles. That figure represents the overall production target, of which the future Japanese facility will account for a share. AIM-120s can cost over $1 million per unit, depending on variant.

The new arrangement also comes after more than two years of discussions over Japanese participation in AMRAAM manufacturing. The initiative emerged in 2024, followed by a feasibility study supported by Mitsubishi Electric and Raytheon. By April 2026, discussions had expanded to include possible Japanese production of circuit card assemblies in addition to final assembly and inspection.

New Production Node For A High-Demand Missile

The AIM-120 beyond-visual-range air-to-air missile is used by a wide range of U.S. and allied combat aircraft, including the F-35. Examples of the stealth fighter are operated by U.S. Air Force and Marine Corps units stationed in Japan, as well as by the Japan Air Self-Defense Force (JASDF). Both the U.S. Air Force and JASDF also operate F-15 variants in Japan, for which AMRAAM is similarly a primary weapon.

A first F-15EX Eagle II designated for Kadena Air Base, Japan, takes off at Boeing’s St. Louis facility. U.S. Air Force photo by Senior Airman Melany Bermudez

AMRAAM is also employed by ground-based air-defense systems, including the National Advanced Surface-to-Air Missile System (NASAMS). This system protects the U.S. Capital Region and over a dozen countries around the globe. It has been extensively battle-proven in Ukraine over nearly three years of operations.

Raytheon says the weapon is fielded by 44 countries and operates across 14 platforms. Its continued importance to fighter operations and air defense has made expanding manufacturing capacity a priority as the United States seeks to replenish inventories and meet soaring international demand. This is in stark contrast to the situation only a few years ago, when global AMRAAM stockpiles were viewed as plentiful.

Since then, AMRAAMs have been used extensively in the U.S. and allied campaign against Houthi drones, as well as in engagements over Syria and Iraq during efforts to defend Israel against multiple Iranian attacks. Ukraine has also expended substantial numbers of these missiles to counter Russian drones and cruise missiles, employing them through NASAMS air defense systems and, more recently, its F-16 fighters. These munitions have been supplied by the United States and NATO allies.

The new U.S. production contract covers Lots 41 through 47 and includes advanced AIM-120D-3 variants featuring modernized processors and software-based electronic-warfare countermeasures.

With a scramble now on to find new ways to increase the global AMRAAM supply, RTX has said it invested in manufacturing equipment, workforce expansion, and supplier capacity to support the production increase. The company also identified new U.S. solid-rocket-motor production lines expected to come online in 2027 as part of its effort to address manufacturing bottlenecks.

Maintainers prepare AIM-120D AMRAAMs for carriage by F-15s during a mass aircraft generation exercise at Kadena Air Base, Japan. U.S. Air Force/Senior Airman Peter Reft

The contract is intended to provide greater production stability than the shorter procurement agreements that previously characterized the program. Its multiyear structure gives Raytheon greater visibility into future demand and supports investment in manufacturing capacity and the missile’s component supply chain.

Japan’s participation could add another assembly location to an expanding industrial base. This should be made easier by the fact that AMRAAM is also less technologically sensitive now that the new AIM-260 is in the works. However, the extent to which the facility will increase total output will depend on its eventual capacity and the division of manufacturing responsibilities between Japanese and U.S. industry.

Technology Transfer And Industrial Safeguards

The agreement is the latest step in a process that has required extensive negotiations over industrial participation and technology protection.

Japan’s longstanding restrictions on arms exports and defense-industrial cooperation have complicated arrangements involving weapons considered offensive in nature. AMRAAM also incorporates classified guidance technology and proprietary intellectual property, requiring safeguards governing the transfer and handling of sensitive information.

The planned facility therefore stops short of a commitment to manufacture the entire missile domestically. Instead, a final assembly and inspection model allows Japan to complete and check missiles using components produced through an industrial arrangement.

For Tokyo, domestic assembly could help support replenishment of its own AMRAAM stocks and strengthen its defense-industrial capabilities. For Washington, an additional qualified production location will diversify the manufacturing network serving U.S. forces and international customers.

SM-3 Production Also In Focus

AMRAAM is not the only missile addressed under Operation Supercharge. Koizumi also confirmed that the United States and Japan had agreed to share technical information intended to increase production of the Standard Missile-3 Block IIA interceptor.

SM-3 Block IIA is designed to intercept ballistic missiles during the midcourse phase of flight and is principally employed by ship-based Aegis missile-defense systems. The interceptor is the product of a joint U.S.-Japanese development program and has also been acquired by Japan, including as the primary effectors for its two new cruiser-sized Aegis System Equipped Vessels (ASEV).

In September, the AI company Anthropic published a blog post that claimed its models were approaching “superhuman capabilities for geolocating outdoor photos”. Anthropic made this claim by comparing its models’ performance to that of players of the popular online game GeoGuessr, which challenges users to geolocate Google Street View images. 

Bellingcat has been running its own benchmark tests on AI models since June 2025, right around the time when Large Language Models (LLMs) began to challenge reverse image searches for assisting with geolocation tasks. In our first round, GPT 4o-mini from OpenAI appeared to be the best tool for geolocating, a title which was taken by Google AI mode when we performed our second round of tests a few months later in August 2025. 

This time, we significantly upped the ante for the models, providing increasingly challenging photos and asking for only one set of coordinates. We tasked not only closed-weight models from Claude, Grok, Gemini, ChatGPT, and Mistral, but also open-weight models from Moonshot AI’s Kimi, commercial provider GeoSeer and the non-generative geolocation model GeoClip. In total, we fed each model 52 photos that had not been published anywhere on the internet before.

The results showed that the current generation of models have markedly improved geolocation capabilities. Anthropic’s Opus and Fable, as well as Google’s recent Gemini 4 Argon model, performed the best on geolocation tasks, narrowing down locations from images faster than a human could under specific conditions. Preliminary tests of a set of agentic skills and tools being developed internally by Bellingcat also showed promising results in further improving the geolocation performance of generic-use AI agents.

But crucially, the tests also showed why humans remain necessary for accurate and trustworthy geolocation work, as no models were foolproof. This is particularly important given the increased use of AI in military contexts. The Anthropic blog post focused specifically on geolocating photos for military and intelligence purposes such as targeting, a historically labour-intensive process requiring significant human expertise. 

While our tests showed some models performed extremely well at narrowing down results to the correct general area, none were close to being perfect at finding the exact spot a picture was taken. That accuracy is extremely important in geolocation, especially in military contexts where the cost of incorrect information can be life or death. It is also important for organisations like Bellingcat whose reputation relies on pinpoint factual accuracy.

Our Methods

To compare model results, we used two metrics built on the distance in kilometres between a prediction and the real location. GeoGuessr Score was used to assess whether models could systematically identify the general area, while the Mean Log Score (explained below) measured which models most accurately pinpointed the correct location in the final few kilometres. 

The GeoGuessr Score metric is based on the online game of the same name, whose scoring system Stanford University academics reverse-engineered in a 2024 study. This system rewards answers close to the general area an image was taken, but does not strongly distinguish between guesses close to the precise location. For example, an answer 200km from the correct location with more points than one 2,000km away. However, an answer 500m from where a picture was taken and another 5km away achieve a similar score. For real-world geolocations, that distance matters a great deal.

For more precision, we introduced a custom metric that grades models higher if they narrow down the search in the last few kilometres. This metric, the Mean Log Score, assigns a value from 0 (completely inaccurate) to 100 (perfect model) and works on a logarithmic scale instead of a linear one like GeoGuessr score.

Therefore, a 10-fold improvement in distance means the same whether it is from 5km to 500m or from 500km to 50km. You can compare both metrics here.

Our Test

To independently assess the models, we sought to give them very specific instructions and some images that were harder to geolocate than those provided in our past tests. The prompt asked for a single set of coordinates reflecting the photographer’s location. The purpose of restricting the response in this way was to prevent hedging, such as multiple guesses or confidence scores, and make the grading more uniform across different models. Models were allowed to use their providers’ built-in tools: search, code execution and page fetch. We capped their token output at 64,000 and ran each model under its default effort setting with a maximum time of 15 minutes per photo. If a model did not return coordinates on the first prompt, we also allowed a single rescue prompt asking it to do so using its previous reasoning and without using any tools.

The images provided were unique, from around the world, not published on the internet before, scrubbed of metadata and provided without any context. They did not contain significant landmarks. We also included images in snowy, foggy and nighttime conditions.

In its blog post, Anthropic claimed its Mythos model beats “even the strongest human baseline on median distance error, scoring 37.0km and 47.2km across 6,000 photos (placing 23.7 percent and 23.1 percent within 1km).“ But Nuno Moniz, Associate Research Professor at the Lucy Family Institute for Data & Society at the University of Notre Dame, told Bellingcat that “the median represents a strong simplification of what would be acceptable to compare AI-based system performance with regards to humans”. 

Anthropic compared its models to competitive GeoGuessr players and their success in identifying the location of Google Street View images. The entire set of test images was online and there was no guarantee the models were processing the imagery for the first time. 

While Anthropic has been relatively open about these methods, not all developers have been as forthcoming. It is therefore difficult to assess whether the same limitations will impact all other models, or whether there will be further caveats that we are simply not aware of. 

Moniz said that “the increasing availability of benchmarking tools and datasets has made it somewhat difficult to measure reliability in test results such as these”. Specifically, due to the ”uncertainty as to whether that data was included in its training”. This is why we ran our Bellingcat tests with non-published photos sourced from Bellingcat staff and the Bellingcat Volunteer Community.

A further limitation of any models comparing performance to GeoGuessr scores is that the game uses images from Google Street View, which is limited to a select number of countries, and within these, a limited number of roads. Street View is also intentionally skewed to feature clear, high-quality photos taken during daytime that are useful for navigation, and users can pan around their view. There are also some well-known tricks that allow GeoGuessr players to bypass actual geolocation to correctly guess the location of photos: for example, Google is known to use a black car in Jordan and a white car in the UAE to capture imagery. While this wouldn’t necessarily impact model performance, it is a drawback of measuring against GeoGuessr score as the game has a set of workarounds that can bypass geolocation skill.

Anthropic’s data consisted of thousands of social media photos of various scenes. Once again, though, these photos were online and may have featured in the model’s training data. Anthropic’s blog post contained four images as an example of this dataset.

The examples Anthropic shared are relatively easy to geolocate as they feature recognisable buildings and signs. But footage that requires geolocation often comes from regions where access is limited and which do not feature on social media or Street View.

Specifically, in a military and intelligence context, footage often involves low-resolution material and intentionally obscured details recorded in remote or inaccessible areas. Context that could be found online can be uncertain or plain wrong, especially during a developing situation, such as the image below that Bellingcat investigators geolocated when verifying a US strike on a school in the city of Minab in Iran that killed at least 120 children earlier this year.

Anthropic researchers acknowledge this caveat, writing that it “does not as clearly emulate the task of precisely pinning down a location in near-real-time in a less populated battlefield setting; that is a task for future research.”

As noted, it is also possible that similar caveats need to be applied to other models when seeking to understand and interpret any geolocation-related benchmarking.

Supercharged Models

In parallel to the above models, we also tested a set of agentic skills and tools being developed internally by Bellingcat. These are researcher-informed instructions and code that can equip generic-use AI agents to augment their geolocation ability. For instance, we designed skills such as allowing it to download Environmental Systems Research Institute (ESRI) satellite imagery tiles. We also designed another skill that enabled direct access to data from Bellingcat’s OpenStreetMaps search tool, which aims to assist geolocation efforts by comparing items identified in imagery (like the position of a pylon compared to a nearby railway line) to OpenStreetMaps data. GeoSleuth is a similar project that has already been published, which we used for comparison. We picked Claude’s Opus 5.5 model to run both skills customisations because of its strong performance and reasonable pricing, and ran all the photos through the supercharged models under the same conditions – except that they had no timeout enforced as the supercharged models required more time to utilise their extra skills. 

The different timeout conditions and expanded toolset do not allow for a direct comparison of the customised models to the previous off-the-shelf models’ results, even if they both outperformed the barebones Opus model they were based on, and all other models on GeoGuessr Score, with only Argon ranking above Bellingcat’s agent in Mean Log Score.

Tests Conclusions

Despite increased model improvement, our analysis shows that important caveats remain and it is important to keep a human in the loop when seeking to guarantee a geolocation is precise and correct.

Our tests show that the coordinates models provide as answers are rarely an exact match, even with supercharged models. Invariably, even the best models, with the best tools, can confidently claim a wrong position. Anthropic says its Mythos model was able to pinpoint less than a quarter of its social media photos dataset within a 1km radius. But narrowing down a location is rarely the trickiest part of geolocation work. A large part of important geolocation work is in the final stretch: finding specific coordinates from a general area. This level of specificity is needed because a general area is not a match. Matching features and perspective are necessary for an accurate geolocation, and in many cases that is done on very low-quality images or even by cross-referencing multiple sources. 

This level of precision is essential for investigations where it matters what building a person could be found at, what lines of sight individuals might have had, or what impact an air strike might have led to. 

Since LLMs can still confidently get things wrong, as our analysis shows, all results still have to be verified independently. Strong human geolocation skills therefore remain crucial, as the more one understands about geolocations, the better one can verify or take to completion an automated result. 

Though it can speed up geolocation work significantly, especially in narrowing down an area of interest, AI models remain a useful assistant rather than a tool we can blindly delegate geolocations to.

The victim’s body was wrapped in quilts, lowered into the trunk of a car and driven about 20 miles southwest of the city, where it was dumped in the woods. The circumstances of his discovery are reduced to five words: “Skeletal remains found in woods.” The case remains unsolved.

Bellingcat spent more than a year investigating in an effort to put a name to the victim. Using open sources, including freedom-of-information requests and newspaper archives, as well as interviews with investigators who worked on the case, we pieced together the story of the man’s life and death in an era before the internet kept a digital record. In total, we submitted 27 public records requests to 18 local, state and federal law enforcement agencies. A Dog with a Bone

On April 10, 1998, a resident in Sharon, an affluent suburb southwest of Boston, called the police. Their black Labrador retriever had returned from the woods carrying a large bone. Four days later, testing by the coroner’s office in Boston determined it was a human femur likely belonging to an adult male. Police searched the woods, where a detective uncovered more bones near a bundle of quilts. Inside, they found a decomposed skeleton. 

A coroner ruled that the victim was a man in his twenties or thirties who was possibly Asian or Native American. He had long black hair with blonde streaks. There were at least two overlapping bullet holes in the back of his skull. Local media reported at the time that the victim had been shot “execution style”. A single, corroded .380 calibre shell casing was found inside the quilt. The victim was wearing a green-and-white striped rugby shirt, denim jeans and white leather sneakers on the night he died. Loose change, a pocket knife and a tarnished set of keys were found nearby on the forest floor.

‘We’ll Kill Your Family’

In the summer of 1996, Chinese restaurant owners and their staff in suburban Boston and New Hampshire were terrorised by a wave of violent home invasions and robberies. Police described the suspects as “an organised group of Asian individuals”.

The modus operandi was often the same. In the early morning hours, young men barged into rooming houses while Chinese restaurant workers slept. Sometimes they robbed the business owners’ homes. One woman who was attacked at knifepoint told police an assailant threatened: “Shut up or I will kill you”. In another case, a victim was stabbed.

The assailants were described as young men or teenagers. In several cases, victims reported that they spoke Fuzhou, also known as Foochow, a language originating from eastern Fujian Province.

It is unclear if the home invasions and robberies were connected, but Bellingcat’s review of historic newspaper clippings and newly released police documents suggest that at least one other gang may have been responsible for some of the crimes.

He said many of these crimes went unreported for cultural reasons, over fears of gang retaliation, and because victims did not trust law enforcement.

“Back then there were many factions of gangs,” he said. “The gang members were recruited throughout the country, nationally and internationally. Law enforcement was always playing catch up to identify the prevalent groups, and the individual members and leaders committing illegal activity.” 

Leong, who is president of the International Organisation of Asian Crime Investigators and Specialists (IOACIS), said authorities had a better understanding of gang culture in Boston by around 1996 when local, state and federal agencies began sharing information.

Murdered in Chinatown

On June 9, 1996, one day after a home invasion in Wolfeboro, New Hampshire, Boston police were called to a Macy’s department store when a man tried to use credit cards stolen in the robbery to buy jewellery and electronics. He was arrested and booked on charges of receiving stolen property. The man was Fu Chun Wang.

In Sharon Police records, Wang was described as “very depressed” and “possibly suicidal”. He told a Boston Police detective that he urgently needed to send $1,000 to his family in China. Other records said his father was ill and needed money. Files found on Judyrecords.com, a free database that purports to include more than 770 million US court case records, show that Wang was arraigned and a court appointed a defence attorney to him. He was bailed out after pleading not guilty. 

Less than two weeks later, on June 21, Wang was reported missing to the Quincy Police Department in Massachusetts. The police report said Wang was last seen in Boston’s Chinatown on June 13, two days after he left jail. Police records indicate that a female friend of Wang’s had asked an attorney to file the missing persons report. The attorney who reported him missing told Bellingcat that he did not remember Wang. 

The Sharon Police Department’s murder case file notes that within weeks of finding the human remains in the woods, they received information from Boston Police and the FBI that “Wang was murdered in Chinatown” in June 1996 and “his body [was] never found”. 

Investigator’s handwritten notes about Wang’s case. Source: Sharon Police Department

Not all documents were released to Bellingcat, and some names were redacted. Fragmentary notes and witness statements provide the only clues to Wang’s final days. In handwritten records, Sharon Police said a man who was described as the “head of [an] Asian gang in Boston” had Wang killed over the New Hampshire breaking-and-entering incident.

A note in the Sharon Police file described a witness to Wang’s murder as a “Flying Dra” and a “NY gangster”. Retired detective Jim Keating confirmed to Bellingcat that Wang, along with his associates and killer, were suspected members of the Fukienese Flying Dragons. He said he believed Wang was murdered because he posed a risk to the gang following his arrest.

Investigator’s notes describing a witness as a gang member. Source: Sharon Police Department

Documents released by the Merrimack Police Department and Sharon Police Department said that an inmate in New York who, in 1997, was serving a 25-year sentence for attempted kidnapping, offered to share information about Wang’s murder with the FBI in return “for a reduction (sentence)”. The inmate implicated the gang in at least two 1996 New Hampshire home invasions and also gave authorities information about the murder.

The account is heavily redacted but includes a note that the US Attorney for New Hampshire was making arrangements to interview the man. Police in New Hampshire said they believed the inmate “was truthful in his statements and has knowledge about the murder of Mr Wang”. Keating confirmed to Bellingcat that he and investigators from other law enforcement agencies traveled to New York to interview the man. He said the inmate, a suspected member of the Fukienese Flying Dragons, gave a statement on Wang’s murder. 

Keating said that investigators had learned of a dispute on the night of the murder between Wang and the gang’s leader. He said Wang had planned to run an illegal gambling operation at an apartment, which the gang boss was using as a prostitution venue. This led to an argument at another location, and when Wang left for the apartment the boss followed him. 

But Keating said that based on the information gathered throughout the investigation, he believes the primary motive for Wang’s murder was his arrest over the use of credit cards stolen in the Wolfeboro home invasion. With that arrest, Wang presented a risk to the rest of the gang because investigators could tie them to the home invasions. “Wang broke the basic rules by taking something that was identifiable and using it, and that’s what the whole damn thing was about,” Keating said.

Crime scene photos of the basement unit on Oxford Place in Boston’s Chinatown where Wang was murdered. Source: Sharon Police Department

Keating told Bellingcat that the crime scene had been damaged by flooding after the murder. He said he used a power saw to cut off the bottom of a door in the unit and that lab testing revealed the presence of Wang’s blood. 

Investigators also worked to confirm Wang’s identity by testing the DNA of the remains found in the woods. In a 1999 case summary written by a Massachusetts State Police Trooper, it was noted that the FBI was attempting to locate Wang’s parents through police in China.

Three months later, the FBI’s Hong Kong office sent a communique to Boston confirming that the Chinese Ministry of Public Security and Interpol had located Wang’s parents. Wang’s mother, it said, was willing to provide a DNA sample for comparison. 

The FBI communique is the final document in the newly released files that details the efforts to confirm Wang’s identity with DNA. However, Keating told Bellingcat that a DNA sample was obtained from Wang’s mother in China. It was brought back to the US for testing and confirmed to be a match, he said. “I don’t know when the DNA was collected or who did it. But I know that they did that,” Keating said. “There is no question about who he was.” 

Bellingcat contacted both the Norfolk and the Suffolk County District Attorneys to confirm the DNA match, but did not receive a response to questions about DNA testing or the victim’s identity. Other former law enforcement officers named in the files released to Bellingcat have not responded to requests for comment. 

One document included in the murder file references discussions between homicide investigators and the FBI about charging Wang’s killer with home invasion offences. The note discusses the potential to have a witness testify against Wang’s suspected killer. It is unknown whether he was ever charged. 

The Shooter

The man authorities suspected of shooting Wang, or ordering his killing, was described in the documents as the head of the Fukienese Flying Dragons in Boston. Police said he was an undocumented immigrant from Fujian Province who ran a sex trafficking ring. 

A redacted note in the Sharon Police Department murder file references Wang’s suspected killer as “a player” in the Chinatown Massacre, an infamous 1991 case in which five men were shot dead in a basement gambling parlour in Boston. In another note in the case file, investigators wrote that he was “thought to be a federal informant”. 

The name of the gang boss was redacted in the police file released to Bellingcat.

Keating said Wang’s identity was confirmed by DNA and that blood evidence, corroborated by witness statements, placed his killing in the Boston Chinatown apartment. The retired Sharon detective said at that point, the Boston Police Department and the Suffolk County District Attorney’s Office should have, respectively, taken over the investigation and prosecution of the case. 

The Boston Police Department did not respond to questions from Bellingcat. It does not include the killing in its list of unsolved homicides. 

The Suffolk County District Attorney’s Office said it did not have records for Wang’s murder. “Unfortunately, after a thorough search with assistance from our homicide unit, no responsive records could be located,” it said.

In response to Bellingcat’s original public records request, the Norfolk County District Attorney’s office, which covers Sharon, said the files were exempt from public disclosure because they pertained to “an active and ongoing criminal investigation”.

A spokesman for the office said last week that the circumstances surrounding the remains of an adult male discovered in Sharon in April 1998 “remain under investigation” by a state police detective assigned to the Norfolk District Attorney’s Unsolved Case Unit. 

The FBI confirmed that the agency assisted state and local partners in an operation investigating home invasions in the late 1990s but did not answer questions about Wang’s murder or his suspected killer. “Given that we’re not the lead, we’ll refer you to Massachusetts State Police,” a spokeswoman said.

Massachusetts State Police referred questions to the Suffolk and Norfolk County District Attorney’s offices.

It remains a mystery why Wang’s killer was never charged and prosecuted for his murder. “They got away with so much. Blatantly got away with so much,” Keating said of the gang. “Killing people for these guys was like … these guys were all expendable.”

The Norfolk District Attorney’s Office encouraged anyone with any information about the case to contact the Massachusetts State Police Tip Line or the Sharon Police Department.
